# Korean War

**Category**: History
**Key figures**: [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md), Syngman Rhee, Douglas MacArthur, Peng Dehuai, Joseph Stalin, Mao Zedong

## Overview

The Korean War (June 25, 1950 – July 27, 1953) was a major armed conflict arising from the post–World War II division of Korea into a Soviet-backed communist north and an American-backed republic in the south. It began with North Korea's invasion of South Korea across the 38th parallel and expanded into an international war involving United Nations forces (led by the United States) and, from October 1950 onward, the Chinese People's Volunteer Army. The war ended in an armistice, not a peace treaty, leaving the peninsula divided along roughly the same line at which it had been divided before the fighting began. The conflict killed an estimated 3–4 million people — military personnel and civilians — making it one of the deadliest wars of the twentieth century.

For North Korea, the war was the defining trauma and formative event of the state. It entrenched militarism, sealed the legitimacy of [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md)'s leadership, and provided the ideological foundation for the DPRK's subsequent isolation and military build-up.

## Background and Origins

Korea had been a Japanese colonial territory from 1910 to 1945. When Japan surrendered on August 15, 1945, American and Soviet forces divided responsibility for disarming Japanese troops at the 38th parallel — the Soviets handling the north, the Americans the south. The division was intended as a temporary administrative convenience, but Cold War antagonism prevented reunification.

By 1948 two separate governments claimed sovereignty over the entire peninsula:

- **The Republic of Korea (ROK)**, inaugurated August 15, 1948, under President Syngman Rhee, recognized by the United Nations.
- **The Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK)**, proclaimed September 9, 1948, under Premier Kim Il-sung, recognized by the Soviet Union and its allies.

Both governments aspired to reunify Korea under their own authority. Border skirmishes along the 38th parallel intensified throughout 1949. The withdrawal of Soviet troops (December 1948) and most US troops (June 1949) left the two Korean armies facing each other with vastly different capabilities. The North Korean People's Army (NKPA) had been built up with Soviet equipment — including approximately 242 T-34 tanks — while the South Korean Army lacked armor and anti-tank weapons.

In early 1950, Kim Il-sung made multiple visits to Moscow and Beijing to secure approval and support for a military campaign to reunify the peninsula. Stalin, initially cautious, gave his conditional approval in January–April 1950, calculating that the United States (which had not included Korea in its stated Pacific defense perimeter in a January 1950 speech by Secretary of State Dean Acheson) would not intervene. Mao Zedong gave his assurance of Chinese support if necessary. Soviet advisors helped finalize the operational plan.

## North Korean Invasion (June 25 – September 1950)

At approximately 4:00 a.m. on **June 25, 1950**, approximately 135,000 NKPA troops crossed the 38th parallel along a broad front, accompanied by Soviet-supplied T-34 tanks. The attack was described by North Korea as a response to a South Korean provocation; the preponderance of evidence shows it was a planned invasion.

Key events of the first weeks:

- **June 28**: Seoul fell to NKPA forces. South Korean defenders destroyed the Han River bridge prematurely, trapping thousands of their own troops north of the river.
- **June 27**: The United Nations Security Council (the Soviet Union boycotting over the China seat) passed a resolution authorizing member states to assist South Korea.
- **June 30**: President Truman authorized US ground forces. The first American unit to arrive was Task Force Smith (a battalion of the 24th Infantry Division), airlifted from Japan.
- **July 5**: Task Force Smith engaged NKPA forces at the **Battle of Osan** — the first US ground engagement of the war. Outgunned and outmaneuvered, the Americans were overrun and pushed south.

Throughout July and August, NKPA forces drove south Korean and US units into an ever-shrinking perimeter around the port of Pusan in the far southeast. By early August, the **Pusan Perimeter** — roughly 140 km by 90 km — was the only territory remaining under UN control. NKPA forces launched repeated assaults to break the perimeter but were held off as US reinforcements and supplies poured in through the port.

## UN Counteroffensive: Inchon and the Advance North (September – October 1950)

General Douglas MacArthur, commander of UN forces, devised a bold amphibious operation to break the strategic deadlock.

**Operation Chromite — Inchon Landing, September 15, 1950:** Against the advice of many military planners (who feared Inchon's extreme tidal range and narrow channel), MacArthur landed the 1st Marine Division and US Army X Corps at the port of Inchon, on Korea's west coast near Seoul. The landing achieved near-complete tactical surprise. Within two weeks, UN forces had cut the NKPA supply lines and encircled large portions of the invading army.

- **September 28, 1950**: Seoul was recaptured by UN forces after four days of urban fighting. MacArthur restored the city to Syngman Rhee with considerable ceremony.
- **September 29–30**: UN forces reached the 38th parallel. The Truman administration authorized MacArthur to cross it and pursue the North Korean forces, with the aim of reunifying Korea under UN auspices.
- **October 1, 1950**: South Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel northward.
- **October 7, 1950**: US and other UN forces crossed the 38th parallel.
- **October 19, 1950**: UN forces captured Pyongyang, the North Korean capital.

Kim Il-sung's government fled to the far north. By late October, advance UN units had reached the Yalu River on the Chinese border, and MacArthur predicted the war would be over by Christmas.

## Chinese Intervention (October 1950 – July 1951)

China had repeatedly warned that it would not permit UN forces to approach its border. On **October 19, 1950**, the Chinese People's Volunteer Army (CPVA), commanded by Marshal Peng Dehuai, began crossing the Yalu River into Korea covertly — marching at night and hiding in forests by day to avoid detection by UN aircraft.

- **October 25, 1950**: First major engagement between Chinese and UN forces near Onjong in northwest Korea. Chinese forces ambushed several ROK divisions, then withdrew. MacArthur and US intelligence initially underestimated the scale of Chinese involvement.
- **November 24, 1950**: MacArthur launched an offensive to end the war by Christmas.
- **November 25–28, 1950**: The Chinese launched a massive two-pronged counteroffensive with approximately 300,000 troops, striking the US Eighth Army in the west and X Corps in the east simultaneously. In the east, Chinese forces encircled the 1st Marine Division and US Army units at the **Chosin Reservoir** (November 27 – December 13, 1950). In temperatures as low as −35°C, the Marines fought their way out in one of the most celebrated fighting retreats in US military history, suffering approximately 4,400 battle casualties and 7,300 non-battle casualties (primarily frostbite) while inflicting estimated 37,500 Chinese casualties.
- **December 5, 1950**: Chinese and North Korean forces retook Pyongyang.
- **January 4, 1951**: Chinese forces captured Seoul for the second time.
- **March 14, 1951**: UN forces retook Seoul for the final time. The war stabilized roughly along the 38th parallel.
- **April 11, 1951**: President Truman relieved MacArthur of command after MacArthur publicly contradicted Truman's policy of limiting the war. General Matthew Ridgway assumed command.

## Stalemate and Armistice Negotiations (1951–1953)

By mid-1951, the front had stabilized roughly along the pre-war border. Both sides recognized that decisive victory was unlikely without unacceptable escalation. Armistice negotiations began on **July 10, 1951**, initially at Kaesong and later at Panmunjom.

The negotiations dragged on for two years, stalled chiefly by the question of **prisoner repatriation**. The US and South Korea insisted on voluntary repatriation — prisoners could choose not to return home — while North Korea and China demanded compulsory repatriation of all prisoners. Tens of thousands of North Korean and Chinese POWs refused repatriation (many having been Nationalist Chinese troops captured by the communists and press-ganged into service). The issue was ultimately resolved through a neutral arbitration mechanism.

While negotiations continued, fighting remained intense. The air war intensified: the US Air Force and Navy conducted a massive bombing campaign that largely destroyed every significant city and industrial facility in North Korea. Pyongyang was bombed so extensively that it was nearly leveled; estimates suggest that the US dropped more bombs on Korea than it had on the Pacific Theater in all of World War II. General Curtis LeMay later estimated that US bombing killed 20 percent of North Korea's population during the war.

Land fighting continued to attrite both sides along the Main Line of Resistance (MLR). Key battles in the stalemate phase included the Battle of Heartbreak Ridge (August–October 1951), the Battle of White Horse (October 1952), and the Battle of Pork Chop Hill (March–July 1953).

## Armistice Agreement (July 27, 1953)

The **Korean Armistice Agreement** was signed at **Panmunjom on July 27, 1953**, by:

- Lieutenant General William K. Harrison Jr. (representing the UN Command)
- General Nam Il (representing the Korean People's Army and Chinese People's Volunteers)

South Korea's Syngman Rhee refused to sign, objecting to a settlement that left Korea divided.

Key provisions:

- An armistice (cease-fire) — not a peace treaty — took effect 12 hours after signing.
- A **Demilitarized Zone (DMZ)** approximately 4 kilometers wide and 250 kilometers long was established along the line of contact, which slightly favored South Korea compared to the 38th parallel.
- A Military Armistice Commission was created to monitor the agreement.
- A Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission (Sweden, Switzerland, Czechoslovakia, Poland) was established to oversee prisoner exchanges and armistice compliance.
- **Operation Big Switch** (August–December 1953) exchanged prisoners: 75,823 North Korean and Chinese POWs vs. 12,773 UN prisoners (of whom 3,597 were American).

No peace treaty has ever been signed. Technically, the Korean War has not ended; the two Koreas remain in a state of armistice.

## Casualties and Destruction

Estimated casualties vary widely by source and category:

| Combatant | Military Dead | Wounded |
|---|---|---|
| United States | ~36,574 | ~103,284 |
| South Korea | ~137,899 | ~450,742 |
| Other UN countries | ~3,094 | ~11,297 |
| North Korea (NKPA) | ~215,000–406,000 | unknown |
| China (CPVA) | ~180,000–400,000 | ~383,000+ |

Civilian deaths were catastrophic. Estimates of Korean civilian deaths (north and south) range from 2 to 3 million. North Korea suffered disproportionately from the massive US air campaign: virtually every city of significance was reduced to rubble, and infrastructure — roads, railways, dams, factories — was systematically destroyed.

The destruction of North Korea's urban and industrial base left a lasting psychological mark on the country's leadership, reinforcing the commitment to self-reliance (juche) and underground military hardening.

## Legacy for North Korea

The Korean War shaped North Korea's subsequent development in several fundamental ways:

**Militarization:** The devastation of the war and the perceived threat of a renewed US-South Korean offensive drove North Korea to build one of the world's largest militaries relative to its population. Defense spending consistently consumed 15–25 percent of GDP, and the [Songun (military-first) policy](../government-politics/government-structure.md) elevated the military to the supreme institution of the state.

**Nuclear deterrence:** The memory of US air power's destruction of North Korean cities directly informed later decisions to pursue nuclear weapons. Kim Il-sung's successors cited the Korean War as evidence that North Korea needed an independent nuclear deterrent to prevent a repeat of 1950–1953.

**Ideology and legitimacy:** Official North Korean historiography presents the war as a defense against a South Korean and US "aggressor" — the "Fatherland Liberation War." This narrative, though at odds with the historical record of North Korean initiation, remains central to the state's legitimacy. The war is portrayed as proof that North Korea can resist imperial aggression and survive.

**Division and DMZ:** The armistice line became permanent. The DMZ — ringed with minefields, guard posts, and armed patrols — is one of the most militarized borders in the world. The continued technical state of war with the United States has been used by the DPRK government to justify political control and mobilization for over seven decades.

**Kim Il-sung's authority:** Having led North Korea through the war and avoided defeat — a far more modest outcome than the original aim of reunification, but framed as victory over imperialism — Kim Il-sung emerged with his authority strengthened and his personality cult amplified.
