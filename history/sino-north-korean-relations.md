# Sino–North Korean Relations

**Category**: History
**Key figures**: [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md), Mao Zedong, Deng Xiaoping, [Kim Jong-il](../people/kim-jong-il.md), [Kim Jong-un](../people/kim-jong-un.md), Xi Jinping

## Overview

Sino–North Korean relations encompass nearly eight decades of shifting alliance, ideological kinship, economic dependence, and strategic tension between the People's Republic of China (PRC) and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK). The relationship is the single most important external factor shaping the DPRK's survival: China accounts for an estimated 90–95% of North Korea's total trade in most years, provides the majority of its energy imports, and is the principal enabler of the regime's ability to circumvent UN Security Council sanctions.

Yet the relationship is also marked by persistent asymmetry (China is vastly more powerful), periodic friction (especially over the [nuclear weapons program](nuclear-weapons-program.md)), and underlying divergence of interest: Beijing prefers a stable, denuclearized Korean peninsula under its influence; Pyongyang prioritizes regime survival and nuclear deterrence above Chinese preferences. This tension has defined the relationship since at least 2006 and constitutes one of the central puzzles of Northeast Asian geopolitics.

---

## Pre-1948: Korean-Chinese Communist Cooperation

The foundations of the relationship predate the PRC itself. During the Japanese colonial period (see [Japanese Colonial Period](japanese-colonial-period.md)), tens of thousands of Koreans lived and fought in Manchuria and northeastern China. [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md) led the anti-Japanese guerrilla operations in Manchuria from the early 1930s, fighting under Chinese communist-affiliated structures (the Northeast Anti-Japanese United Army) and maintaining close ties with Chinese communist cadres. Several future DPRK military leaders — including Choe Yong-gon and Kim Chaek — fought alongside the Chinese communist forces in the Chinese Civil War (1945–1949), returning to Korea to form the core of the Korean People's Army in 1948.

This shared history of armed struggle created a personal solidarity network at the founding generation level, and gave the DPRK's leadership experience of Chinese communist military methods and political culture that shaped early bilateral ties.

## The Korean War and the "Blood Alliance" (1950–1953)

The defining moment of Sino–North Korean relations was China's entry into the [Korean War](korean-war.md) in October 1950. After UN forces under General Douglas MacArthur crossed the 38th parallel and advanced toward the Yalu River (China's border), Mao Zedong committed the Chinese People's Volunteer Army (CPVA) to Korea in late October 1950, over the objections of several Politburo members who feared provoking US nuclear retaliation.

Key facts:
- Between 1950 and 1953, approximately **2.3 million Chinese troops** served in Korea
- Chinese casualties are estimated at **180,000–200,000 killed**, including Mao's own son, Mao Anying, killed in a US air strike in November 1950 — a sacrifice that gave the relationship a personal dimension for Mao
- Chinese intervention reversed the UN advance and established the armistice line close to the original 38th parallel
- The CPVA provided critical combat power that prevented the DPRK's military defeat and established the principle — enshrined in the 1961 **Sino–North Korean Treaty of Friendship, Co-operation, and Mutual Assistance** — that China would intervene militarily to prevent North Korea's conquest

The 1961 Treaty remains formally in force. Article 2 commits China to immediately providing "military and other assistance by all means" if North Korea is "subjected to the armed attack of any single state or any coalition of states" — a mutual defense clause that has shaped US military planning on the Korean peninsula for decades.

The Korean War experience was memorialized in the DPRK as proof of Chinese brotherly sacrifice; the phrase "lips and teeth" (순망치한, 唇亡齒寒 — "if the lips are gone, the teeth feel the cold") became the standard formulation of the relationship's mutual necessity.

## Ideological Alliance and Sino–Soviet Competition (1953–1972)

During the 1950s and early 1960s, the DPRK maintained close alignment with both the Soviet Union and China, receiving reconstruction aid from both after the Korean War. The Sino–Soviet split (beginning circa 1960) forced the DPRK into a difficult balancing act: both Moscow and Beijing sought to draw Pyongyang into their respective camps.

Kim Il-sung's strategy was skillful non-alignment between the communist giants:
- **1956 August Faction Incident**: Kim Il-sung purged pro-Soviet and pro-Chinese factions within the WPK, asserting complete independence from both external communist powers
- Through the 1960s, the DPRK oscillated in its public alignment, often tilting toward Beijing during periods of heightened Chinese economic support and toward Moscow when Soviet military hardware deliveries mattered more
- The [Juche](../culture-society/state-ideology-and-propaganda.md) ideology (crystallized in the 1972 constitution) was partly a response to these competing external pressures: asserting Korean self-reliance as a counter to dependence on either Moscow or Beijing

The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) severely strained Sino–DPRK ties. Red Guards denounced Kim Il-sung as a "fat revisionist," and China temporarily reduced support. Relations were restored after the Cultural Revolution's worst phase subsided.

## Normalization Shock: China Recognizes South Korea (1992)

After Mao's death and Deng Xiaoping's rise to power, China pursued economic modernization (Reform and Opening, 1978) and gradually normalized relations with Western countries and eventually South Korea. Beijing's decision to establish diplomatic relations with the Republic of Korea in **August 1992** was a profound shock to Pyongyang:

- China recognized the ROK government (and South Korea recognized the PRC), signaling that Beijing's economic interests and its strategic relationship with the United States outweighed ideological solidarity with the DPRK
- The DPRK was not consulted; Kim Il-sung reportedly sent a furious message to Beijing after the announcement
- The recognition meant China now maintained formal ties with both Korean states, fundamentally altering the bilateral dynamic

The 1992 shock accelerated the DPRK's sense of isolation and its drive toward nuclear weapons as the ultimate insurance against regime collapse. It also ended the period of quasi-ideological partnership: from 1992 onward, the relationship became primarily transactional — China valued the DPRK as a buffer state and strategic card against US influence in Northeast Asia, while the DPRK valued China as its economic lifeline and diplomatic shield.

## China as Economic Lifeline (1992–2010)

The simultaneous collapse of Soviet aid (1991) and China's reduction of subsidized trade (China began billing the DPRK at market prices for oil and food in 1993) contributed directly to the economic catastrophe of the mid-1990s. The [famine](../economy/economic-system-and-famine.md) of 1994–1998, which killed an estimated 250,000–600,000 people, was worsened by the loss of external food and fuel support.

However, China never severed trade entirely. Border trade through the Sinuiju–Dandong crossing and the Tumen River area (see [Rason](../geography/rason.md)) provided a floor beneath which the DPRK economy could not fall. From the late 1990s onward, China became the dominant trade partner by an overwhelming margin:

| Year | DPRK–China Trade (est.) | % of Total DPRK Trade |
|------|------------------------|------------------------|
| 2000 | ~$490 million | ~25% |
| 2005 | ~$1.6 billion | ~53% |
| 2010 | ~$3.5 billion | ~83% |
| 2015 | ~$5.5 billion | ~92% |
| 2019 | ~$2.7 billion | ~95% (post-sanctions reduction) |

Chinese exports to the DPRK (food, fuel, machinery, consumer goods) and imports from the DPRK (coal, iron ore, seafood, textiles) were the primary arteries of North Korean economic activity. The [mining and resources](../economy/mining-and-resources.md) sector — particularly coal — was the DPRK's main hard-currency earner via China until UN Security Council Resolution 2371 (August 2017) banned coal imports.

## Nuclear Tests and Chinese Pressure (2006–2017)

China's most significant points of friction with the DPRK have been over nuclear weapons. Each of the DPRK's six nuclear tests prompted Chinese condemnation and, to varying degrees, cooperation with UN sanctions:

- **Test 1 (October 2006)**: China condemned the test as "flagrant," voted for UNSCR 1718 (imposing initial sanctions), and temporarily tightened border controls — though implementation was inconsistent
- **Test 2 (May 2009)**: China again condemned but moderated the sanctions response, resisting measures that would threaten regime stability
- **Tests 3–4 (2013, January 2016)**: Growing Chinese frustration, particularly after the January 2016 test was conducted without advance warning to Beijing — a deliberate snub that angered Xi Jinping
- **Tests 5–6 (September 2016, September 2017)**: China voted for UNSCR 2270 (March 2016), 2321 (November 2016), 2356, 2371, 2375, and 2397 (2017) — a significant escalation of sanctions including coal import bans and limits on North Korean workers abroad. Chinese banks began cutting off DPRK-linked accounts under US financial pressure

Yet throughout this period, China never cut off oil supplies (the DPRK imports approximately **500,000–550,000 tons** of crude oil annually, almost entirely from China) and maintained basic food trade, drawing a line at measures that would destabilize the regime.

### THAAD Crisis (2016–2017)

South Korea's July 2016 decision to deploy the US Terminal High Altitude Area Defense (THAAD) anti-missile system prompted Chinese economic retaliation against South Korea (restricting Chinese tourism, boycotting Korean entertainment, pressuring Korean companies). This episode revealed that China's primary strategic concern in Korea was not North Korean behavior but US military presence — and that China would use economic coercion against South Korea before escalating pressure on the DPRK.

## Diplomatic Re-engagement and the Kim Jong-un Era

[Kim Jong-un](../people/kim-jong-un.md) did not visit China until March 2018 — more than six years after assuming power — reflecting the depth of DPRK–China diplomatic estrangement over the nuclear program. Between March 2018 and January 2019, Kim Jong-un made four visits to China (meeting Xi Jinping each time — in Beijing, Dalian, and Beijing again in 2018, and Beijing once more in January 2019), and Xi reciprocated with a state visit to Pyongyang in June 2019. These meetings bracketed Kim's summits with South Korean President Moon Jae-in and his meetings with US President Donald Trump (Singapore, June 2018; Hanoi, February 2019; the DMZ, June 2019).

China welcomed the diplomatic opening as consistent with its long-stated preference for dialogue over military pressure, and used its restored relationship with Pyongyang to reassert its indispensable role in any Korean peninsula settlement — countering the US narrative that China had become marginalized by direct US–DPRK diplomacy.

After the collapse of the Hanoi summit (February 2019), the DPRK returned to ballistic missile testing and nuclear threats. China moderated its sanctions enforcement further, and the COVID-19 border closure (January 2020 – late 2023, when the DPRK unilaterally sealed its borders) paradoxically deepened North Korea's isolation while giving it an excuse to reset trade terms upon reopening.

---

## Post-2022: Russia Pivot and Continuing Chinese Dependence

Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 created a new alignment dynamic. The DPRK quickly supported Russia diplomatically and, by 2022–2023, reportedly began supplying artillery shells, missiles, and other munitions to Russia. Kim Jong-un's September 2023 visit to Russia (meeting President Vladimir Putin at the Vostochny Cosmodrome) and a subsequent June 2024 Putin visit to Pyongyang cemented a new DPRK–Russia axis, formalized in the **June 2024 Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty**, which includes a mutual defense clause (Article 4) similar to the 1961 DPRK–China treaty.

Reports from South Korean, US, and European intelligence agencies assessed that approximately **10,000–12,000 North Korean troops** were deployed to Russia's Kursk region by late 2024, the most significant deployment of KPA forces outside the peninsula since the Vietnam War. This deepened the Russia relationship gave the DPRK an alternative patron for energy, food, and political support.

Yet China remains irreplaceable as an economic lifeline:
- Russia's ability to supply the volume and variety of goods China provides is limited
- The DPRK's [railway and transportation network](../geography/railway-and-transportation-networks.md) infrastructure for cross-border trade is oriented primarily toward China
- China's position in the UN Security Council (permanent veto power) is essential to blocking additional sanctions

Beijing has responded to the DPRK–Russia deepening with public neutrality but private concern: a more autonomous DPRK that earns hard currency and military technology from Russia reduces Chinese leverage, though it also burdens Russia with supporting costs China no longer bears alone.

## Structural Dynamics

### China's Strategic Calculation

China's tolerance of the DPRK despite its nuclear weapons program and periodic provocations reflects several calculations:

1. **Buffer state value**: A North Korean collapse or unification under a US-allied government would bring US troops to China's border — an outcome Beijing views as strategically intolerable
2. **Sanctions evasion facilitation**: Chinese tolerance for DPRK-linked shell companies, money transfers through Liaoning and Jilin province banks, and informal cross-border trade has been the primary enabler of DPRK sanctions evasion (documented extensively in UN Panel of Experts reports)
3. **Leverage preservation**: China's ability to increase or decrease trade pressure is its primary instrument of leverage over Pyongyang; cutting off the DPRK entirely would eliminate that leverage
4. **US relationship management**: China uses the DPRK file as a bargaining chip in Sino–US negotiations, offering greater enforcement pressure in exchange for concessions on trade, Taiwan, or other issues

### The DPRK's Asymmetric Leverage

Despite its vastly inferior power, the DPRK is not simply a Chinese client state. Pyongyang's leverage includes:

- **Threat of collapse**: A DPRK implosion would create a refugee crisis and potentially a unified pro-US Korea — the scenario China most wants to prevent
- **Military escalation**: Missile tests and nuclear threats force Chinese diplomatic energy and create costs for Sino–US relations, giving the DPRK negative leverage
- **Diversification**: The Russia relationship (post-2022) reduces the DPRK's dependence on China marginally but meaningfully, reducing Beijing's monopoly on external support
- **Historical narrative**: The "blood alliance" mythology gives the DPRK moral standing in bilateral negotiations that purely transactional economic analysis would miss
