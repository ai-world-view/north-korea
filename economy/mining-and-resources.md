# Mining and Resource Extraction

**Category**: Economy  
**Key figures**: KPA mining operations; General Bureau of Atomic Energy; resource traders; international sanctions bodies

## Summary

Mining and resource extraction have been central to the DPRK's economy since its founding, both as sources of hard currency and as inputs to heavy industry and the military-industrial complex. North Korea possesses significant mineral reserves: coal, iron ore, copper, zinc, lead, magnesium, molybdenum, rare earth elements, and uranium. The regime developed an extensive mining sector under Soviet tutelage, initially extracting resources for export to the USSR and Eastern Bloc and for domestic heavy industry.

The [Korean People's Army](../government-politics/korean-peoples-army.md) operates vast mining enterprises, particularly coal mines, as a core economic conglomerate function. KPA mining units are staffed with soldiers, prison laborers from [kwanliso camps](../culture-society/kwanliso.md), and drafted civilians, making mining a mechanism of both economic production and internal social control. Mines suffer from chronic lack of safety equipment, inadequate ventilation, dangerous working conditions, and minimal health standards.

Coal mining is the largest extraction sector. North Korea exports coal to China (its primary customer), generating critical hard currency. [International sanctions](foreign-trade-and-sanctions.md) have constrained coal exports, but smuggling networks continue to move coal to international buyers. Uranium mining and processing form a separate strategic sector tied to the [nuclear weapons program](../history/nuclear-weapons-program.md).

## Mineral Wealth: An Overview

North Korea is estimated to hold some of the world's largest untapped mineral reserves. South Korean government analyses, particularly from the Korea Resources Corporation (KORES) and the Korea Institute of Geoscience and Mineral Resources (KIGAM), have estimated the total economic value of North Korean mineral deposits at approximately $6–10 trillion (USD) as of the 2010s — an assessment that, if accurate, would rank the DPRK among the world's most mineral-rich countries per capita.

Key mineral categories include:

- **Coal** — the largest sector by volume and value; both anthracite (hard coal) and bituminous deposits
- **Iron ore** — in the Musan region of North Hamgyong Province, one of the largest iron ore deposits in East Asia
- **Copper, zinc, and lead** — significant polymetallic deposits, particularly in South Pyongan and North Pyongan provinces
- **Magnesite** — estimated reserves of approximately 6 billion tons, among the world's largest; the Tanchon deposit in South Hamgyong Province is a primary site
- **Molybdenum** — significant deposits in North Pyongan Province
- **Graphite** — mined for domestic use in electrodes and refractories and for export
- **Rare earth elements (REEs)** — the Jongju deposit in South Pyongan Province, identified by KIGAM estimates, may contain approximately 216 million metric tons of REE oxides, potentially one of the world's largest rare earth deposits; however, independent verification is impossible given access restrictions
- **Uranium** — significant reserves supporting the nuclear weapons program; estimated at approximately 26 million metric tons of uranium ore, concentrated in deposits in South Pyongan, North Hamgyong, and other provinces

Despite this nominal wealth, the DPRK's mining sector operates far below its geological potential. Chronic [electricity shortages](energy-crisis-and-electricity.md), lack of modern equipment, absence of foreign investment under sanctions, and the use of forced labor reduce productivity dramatically. Much of the country's mineral wealth remains in the ground, inaccessible to an economy lacking the capital, technology, and energy to extract it efficiently.

## Coal: The Dominant Sector

Coal is the DPRK's most important mineral resource by volume extracted, domestic utility, and export revenue. The country produces both anthracite (used for residential heating and some export) and bituminous coal (used for coking in steel production). Major coal fields are located in:

- **South Pyongan Province** — the Sunchon, Kaechon (Anju), and Tokchon coal basins are among the most productive; the Sunchon and North Pyongan fields supply Pyongyang and the chemical industry
- **North Hamgyong Province** — coalfields near Aoji (Hoeryong area) supply northeastern industrial cities including [Hamhung](../geography/hamhung.md) and Chongjin
- **South Hamgyong Province** — the Hamhung chemical complex historically depended on coal from regional mines

Coal production peaked in the 1980s at an estimated 30–40 million tons per year under Soviet-era investment. Post-Soviet collapse brought output down precipitously; by the mid-1990s, during the famine, production may have fallen to as low as 15–18 million tons per year. Under Kim Jong-un, coal production reportedly recovered to approximately 20–30 million tons per year by the mid-2010s, driven by exports to China.

**Export dependency:** From approximately 2013 to 2017, coal exports to China generated the DPRK's single largest source of hard-currency revenue. Estimates from Chinese customs data and UN sanctions monitoring reports indicate that North Korea exported approximately 20–22 million tons of coal to China in peak years (2015–2016), generating roughly $1.0–1.2 billion USD annually. China's share as the buyer was close to 100% of declared exports.

UN Security Council Resolution 2371 (August 2017), adopted in response to the Hwasong-14 ICBM test, banned all North Korean coal exports. This measure — the most economically significant single sanction against the DPRK — eliminated the legal export market overnight. China nominally enforced the ban; official Chinese import data shows coal imports from North Korea dropping to near-zero after 2017. However, UN Panel of Experts reports and satellite-based maritime tracking have documented ongoing coal smuggling through ship-to-ship transfers in international waters, where coal is offloaded to vessels that falsify flag, destination, and cargo manifests before delivering to Chinese, Vietnamese, and other ports. The actual post-2017 coal smuggling volume is difficult to quantify but is estimated in the range of several million tons annually.

## Iron, Copper, and Metals

The Musan iron ore mine in North Hamgyong Province, near the Chinese border, is one of the largest open-cast iron mines in East Asia, with ore reserves estimated in the billions of tons. Under Chinese investment agreements from the early 2000s, Chinese companies obtained preferential access to Musan's output in exchange for infrastructure investment and processing capacity. The relationship exemplified the DPRK's strategy of exchanging mineral access for Chinese capital while maintaining formal sovereignty over resources.

Copper, zinc, and lead mining at sites including the Ryongyang and Onsong deposits in North Hamgyong, and the Junggang area in Chagang Province, supply both domestic metallurgical needs and export markets. These metals are more easily smuggled than coal — in smaller, higher-value shipments — and have continued to reach international markets despite sanctions, typically through Chinese intermediaries and falsely labeled goods.

The Tanchon special economic zone, designated in South Hamgyong Province, was intended to attract foreign investment in mineral processing; it has remained largely undeveloped due to sanctions and the DPRK's inability to guarantee regulatory stability.

## Rare Earth Elements

The potential significance of North Korea's rare earth reserves has attracted international attention since the Jongju deposit was widely reported around 2012–2013. If the KIGAM estimates are accurate, the Jongju deposit alone could represent reserves comparable to or exceeding China's Bayan Obo mine — China's current dominant source of global REE production. Rare earths are critical inputs to electric motors, wind turbines, and consumer electronics, making them strategically important.

However, the DPRK's ability to monetize these reserves is severely constrained by:
- Lack of REE processing and refining technology (which China dominates globally)
- UN sanctions prohibiting exports of DPRK mineral commodities
- Infrastructure deficit — the deposits are in remote areas without adequate road, rail, or power connections
- The requirement for large capital investment the regime cannot attract under sanctions conditions

As of the mid-2020s, systematic REE extraction and export from North Korea remained largely unrealized, despite its potential as a future revenue source.

## Uranium Mining and the Nuclear Program

Uranium is a strategically sensitive mineral given its role in the [nuclear weapons program](../history/nuclear-weapons-program.md). The DPRK operates uranium mines in multiple provinces — with significant deposits reported in South Pyongan, North Hamgyong, and possibly Hwanghae and South Hamgyong — and processes uranium ore at the Yongbyon Nuclear Scientific Research Center and other undisclosed facilities.

The General Bureau of Atomic Energy (GBAE), formerly the General Department of Atomic Energy, oversees the nuclear fuel cycle from mining to enrichment. Uranium mining and enrichment production figures are classified state secrets. International intelligence assessments and satellite imagery analysis by 38 North, the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), and the IAEA suggest the DPRK has accumulated sufficient fissile material for approximately 40–50 nuclear weapons as of the mid-2020s, implying substantial ongoing uranium mining and enrichment.

The uranium program's intersection with mining means that resource extraction is directly tied to regime survival and strategic deterrence. The Kim Jong-un government's announcement in March 2013 of the "Byungjin Line" — simultaneous development of nuclear weapons and the economy — explicitly positioned uranium and nuclear development as inseparable from economic strategy.

## Labor Conditions and Human Rights

Mining labor is one of the DPRK's most brutal work environments. Coal mines, metal mines, and quarries employ a combination of:

- **KPA soldiers** — military units operate entire mines as economic enterprises under the KPA's General Logistics Bureau and other organs; soldiers are not paid wages in any meaningful sense
- **Kwanliso political prisoners** — inmates at [political prison camps](../culture-society/kwanliso.md), including Camp 14 (Kaechon/Gaechon), Camp 15 (Yodok), and Camp 16 (Hwasong), are assigned mining labor under conditions of forced servitude; they receive minimal rations, no safety equipment, and no compensation
- **Drafted civilians** — under the "loyalty labor" system, civilians are periodically assigned to mining work through the WPK's mass mobilization structures

Mine safety is chronically inadequate. Ventilation equipment is absent or non-functional; roof supports are inadequate; miners work without respirators in coal mines where pneumoconiosis (black lung disease) is endemic. Mine explosions and collapses are not publicly reported by DPRK state media but are documented through defector testimony. Miners at kwanliso mines have no recourse for injury; those killed in accidents are simply replaced from the prison population.

The North Korean Human Rights Database Center (NKDB) in Seoul, HRNK, and other organizations have compiled defector testimony specifically on mining labor conditions. Survivors of Camp 14 at Kaechon — a coal-mining camp — describe conditions of near-starvation combined with dangerous extraction work, with death rates among prisoners extremely high.

## Sanctions Evasion Networks

International sanctions targeting North Korean mineral exports — particularly coal, iron, iron ore, lead, lead ore, and seafood under the 2017 resolutions — have generated a sophisticated and well-documented evasion industry. The UN Panel of Experts reports (annual since 2010, expanded after 2016) document the following primary evasion techniques:

- **Ship-to-ship (STS) transfers** — North Korean-operated or -chartered vessels offload coal and other cargo to "clean" vessels at sea, often at night or in bad weather, with AIS transponders disabled; the receiving vessel then proceeds to port with falsified documentation indicating a different cargo origin
- **Falsified invoices and certificates of origin** — coal and metals are relabeled as coming from third countries (Russia, Mongolia, Vietnam) in paperwork submitted to port authorities
- **Shell company networks** — front companies in multiple jurisdictions (often Hong Kong, Singapore, and Southeast Asian ports) handle transactions to obscure DPRK ownership of the cargo
- **Consolidated cargo** — North Korean goods are mixed with legitimate cargo from third countries to reduce detectability
- **Russian facilitation** — following the 2022 DPRK-Russia strategic partnership (see [nuclear weapons program](../history/nuclear-weapons-program.md) and [foreign trade and sanctions](foreign-trade-and-sanctions.md)), Russia has become a more active facilitator of sanctions evasion, using its veto at the UN Security Council to shield the DPRK from enforcement actions

## Significance

Mining is a fundamental revenue source for the regime under sanctions. When conventional trade, manufacturing, and agriculture have failed due to sanctions, [energy crisis](energy-crisis-and-electricity.md), and mismanagement, mining remains a commodity the regime can extract and sell, particularly to China. Mining revenue funds military modernization and the regime's survival.

Mining also exemplifies the regime's exploitation of labor as an economic strategy. By using military conscripts, prison laborers, and drafted civilians, the regime avoids paying wages equivalent to the work's economic value. Kwanliso camp prisoners have no rights, no choice of labor, and receive minimal subsistence; their deaths reduce the regime's burden rather than creating losses. Mining thus subsidizes both the military and the security apparatus through forced-labor arbitrage.

The nuclear-weapons program's dependence on uranium mining ties resource extraction directly to strategic deterrence. The regime's investment in uranium mining reflects its prioritization of nuclear weapons over civilian welfare; uranium extraction diverts labor, capital, and technical expertise from civilian [energy](energy-crisis-and-electricity.md) and other economic needs.

International sanctions specifically target North Korean coal and mineral exports, recognizing their centrality to regime finances. The sanctions war on mineral exports reveals how completely the regime depends on extractive industries for survival in isolation.
