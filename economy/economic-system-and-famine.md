# Economic System and Famine

**Category**: Economy
**Key figures**: [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md), Kim Jong-il
**Period**: 1948–present; famine 1994–1998 (acute phase)

## Overview

North Korea operates under a centrally planned socialist economy characterized by state ownership of all significant means of production, collective agriculture, and directed industrial development. The economy has been in structural decline since the collapse of Soviet aid in the early 1990s, precipitating a catastrophic famine (1994–1998) that killed an estimated 600,000–2 million people. Chronic shortages of food, electricity, and basic goods persist, and the economy relies on international aid, remittances from diaspora communities, and illegal markets to function.

The architecture of the DPRK economy reflects Marxist-Leninist principles adapted to Kim Il-sung's juche ideology: the state controls production, distribution, and consumption. In theory, the economy aims at autarky (economic self-sufficiency) and prioritizes heavy industry and military production. In practice, it has proven unable to feed its own population or maintain basic infrastructure without external support.

## Post-War Economic System (1953–1989)

After the Korean War, North Korea undertook rapid industrialization under Soviet and Chinese economic assistance and technical expertise. The early post-war period (1953–1970s) saw genuine economic growth:

**Heavy Industrialization** — The **Chollima Movement** (1956–1963), named after a mythical winged horse in Korean legend, was a mass mobilization campaign modeled on Soviet Stakhanovism. It aimed to accelerate industrial production through campaigns of competitive overfulfillment of quotas. Steel mills, coal mines, chemical plants, and hydroelectric dams were built with Soviet and Chinese support.

**Collectivized Agriculture** — All agricultural land was organized into state farms (sovkhoses) and agricultural cooperatives (kolkhozes). Peasants worked collectively under state-appointed managers, with production quotas set by central planning. The state appropriated most production for distribution through the Public Distribution System (PDS) and export; individual peasant plots were minimal and tightly controlled.

**Central Planning** — A State Planning Commission set five-year and annual production targets. Ministries managed industrial sectors; provincial administrations managed regional agricultural output. All major economic decisions flowed from the top leadership, with little feedback from actual conditions or worker input.

**Foreign Trade** — North Korea engaged in trade primarily with the Soviet Union, China, and the Soviet bloc (East Germany, Poland, Czechoslovakia). It exported minerals, agricultural products, and light manufactures in exchange for fuel, machinery, and raw materials.

**Military-Industrial Complex** — From [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md)'s era onward, a substantial share of industrial capacity was dedicated to military production: ammunition, weapons, vehicles, and eventually missiles and nuclear components. Military production has consistently claimed 20–25 percent of GDP, far above international norms.

The economy grew at moderate rates during this early phase (estimated 5–8 percent annually in the 1950s–1960s, declining to 2–3 percent by the 1980s), but it grew from a very low base and was artificially stimulated by external aid.

## Economic Stagnation (1970s–1980s)

By the 1970s, growth rates slowed. Several structural problems became apparent:

- **Technological lag** — North Korea could not match South Korea's technological advances or manufacturing quality. Its industrial base became outdated relative to global standards.
- **Energy constraints** — North Korea lacked significant oil resources and depended on imported Soviet petroleum. Rising global oil prices in the 1970s and 1980s strained finances.
- **Declining aid** — Soviet and Chinese economic assistance began declining in real terms as both countries faced their own economic pressures and began normalizing relations with South Korea.
- **Agricultural inefficiency** — Collective agriculture produced insufficient yields. The state maintained quotas regardless of actual harvests, leading to rural food shortages even in pre-famine years.
- **Export decline** — North Korean manufactures were not competitive on global markets. Export earnings fell as demand for its products declined.

**Kim Jong-il's Reforms (1980s)** — The younger Kim Jong-il, gradually assuming leadership responsibilities, introduced limited market mechanisms and opened special economic zones (e.g., Rajin-Sonbong, later Rason). These experiments had modest effects; the fundamental structural problems remained.

## The Soviet Collapse and Cascading Crisis (1989–1994)

The collapse of the Soviet Union (1991) and the disintegration of Soviet-bloc economies had catastrophic effects on North Korea:

- **End of Soviet aid** — Subsidized Soviet oil, grain shipments, and technical assistance ceased abruptly.
- **Loss of trading partners** — East German, Polish, and other Soviet-bloc trade partners vanished as these countries transitioned to market economies.
- **Foreign exchange crisis** — North Korea had accumulated hard-currency debt and had few means to service it or earn new foreign exchange.
- **Domestic production collapse** — Without imported fuel, fertilizer, and spare parts, North Korean industry ground to a halt. Fertilizer shortages devastated agriculture.

The state attempted to compensate through austerity and further isolation, but these measures proved insufficient. Agricultural production fell sharply: 1990, 1991, 1992 saw harvest shortfalls, but the state continued to extract quotas from farmers, leaving insufficient grain for seed stock and human consumption.

## The Famine (1994–1998)

**Onset** — By late 1993 and early 1994, food shortages had become severe. The Public Distribution System, already rationing reduced amounts, began to fail in some regions. The state's inability to import grain or fertilizer, combined with agricultural collectivization's structural inefficiency, created a perfect crisis. In August 1995, catastrophic floods inundated 400,000 hectares of farmland in the western provinces — in official accounts, these floods became the regime's primary explanation for the famine, obscuring the structural causes. Drought in 1997 compounded the agricultural failure.

**"The Arduous March"** — The regime labelled this period the **고난의 행군** (Arduous March), borrowing a phrase from Kim Il-sung's guerrilla campaigns to frame collective suffering as a revolutionary test of loyalty and perseverance. Citizens were expected to endure hunger as a patriotic duty. State media simultaneously denied the crisis existed and called on citizens to fight on in the face of "imperialist blockade."

**Deaths** — The famine was catastrophic. Estimates of deaths range from 600,000 to 3.5 million; the most credible scholarly consensus places the death toll at approximately 600,000–1 million (2.5–5 percent of North Korea's population of ~23 million at the time). North Korean demographic studies conducted after 1998 have estimated excess mortality at roughly 660,000. The exact toll will never be known, as the North Korean state did not release detailed mortality statistics and suppressed information about the famine.

**Social Impact** — The famine affected different regions unevenly. Rural areas and provinces distant from [Pyongyang](../geography/pyongyang.md) suffered disproportionately. Urban centers, particularly Pyongyang, received preferential distribution and suffered less acute hunger. The northeast — North Hamgyong and South Hamgyong provinces, furthest from the capital and most dependent on Soviet-era industrial employment — experienced the highest death rates. Some estimates suggest 10–15 percent of the population in these provinces died during the acute years.

Families were separated as people fled to find food or were forcibly relocated by the state. Orphanages overflowed with **"kotjebi"** (꽃제비, literally "swallows that wander for flowers" — homeless street children whose families had perished or abandoned them). Instances of cannibalism were reported by defectors and international humanitarian workers, though the scale of such practices is uncertain. An estimated 100,000–300,000 North Koreans crossed the Chinese border seeking food and refuge during the peak famine years; a significant fraction remained as unregistered migrants, subject to deportation back to North Korea.

**State Response** — [Kim Jong-il](../people/kim-jong-il.md)'s government initially denied the severity of the crisis and resisted international humanitarian assistance. In 1995, the regime reluctantly accepted emergency aid and permitted a limited UN presence. The World Food Programme (WFP) operation expanded rapidly: between 1995 and 2003, the WFP alone delivered an estimated 1.8 million metric tons of food aid. South Korea provided substantial bilateral aid, especially after the June 2000 inter-Korean summit. However, aid was insufficient to prevent mass starvation, access was severely restricted (no independent monitoring in military zones, selected regions), and the state continued to divert aid toward party elites and the military.

**International Dimension** — Despite the famine, North Korea continued military spending and actively pursued its nuclear weapons program. In 1996, 1997, and 1998, North Korea conducted missile tests (Rodong-1, Taepodong-1 in August 1998). The US, South Korea, China, and international organizations provided food aid estimated collectively at over $2 billion between 1995 and 2005, but political tensions (especially around the nuclear program) repeatedly interrupted aid flows. The diversion of aid to non-civilian beneficiaries, and the regime's systematic denial of access to humanitarian workers, drew international criticism throughout the relief operation.

## Post-Famine Economy (1998–Present)

After the acute famine phase (1998 onward), North Korea's economy has stabilized at a very low level. Key features:

**Continued Stagnation** — Growth rates have averaged 0–2 percent annually, often negative. Per-capita GDP is estimated at $1,000–$2,000 (far below South Korea's $30,000+), and the economy remains essentially non-competitive on global markets.

**Informal Markets** — The famine was a turning point in the emergence of private commerce. As state distribution collapsed, citizens improvised survival through barter and small-scale trade. These **jangmadang** (장마당, literally "market grounds") proliferated throughout the late 1990s and 2000s, evolving from desperate food exchange to complex markets trading in imported Chinese consumer goods, electronics, information (black-market DVDs and USB drives containing South Korean dramas), and even services. By the 2010s, hundreds of official or semi-official markets were operating in every major city. In 2003, the regime partially legalized these markets (the July 2002 reforms introduced limited price and wage adjustments), though periodic crackdowns — including Kim Jong-il's disastrous November 2009 currency redenomination, which wiped out private savings and triggered widespread unrest — demonstrated the state's ambivalence toward marketization.

**Foreign Exchange Crisis** — North Korea has pursued various strategies to earn hard currency: counterfeiting, drug trafficking, weapons sales (missile technology to Middle Eastern states), and forced labor camps producing goods for export. These activities, while generating some revenue, are limited and come with severe international consequences.

**Sanctions and Isolation** — International sanctions (imposed after nuclear and missile tests) have further constrained the economy. Major trading partners are restricted, and North Korea's ability to access international credit markets is nearly non-existent.

**Agricultural Dependence** — North Korea remains unable to feed itself. Food aid from international organizations and countries (particularly China and South Korea, irregularly provided) remains necessary to prevent renewed mass starvation. Annual harvests fail to meet domestic needs; the Public Distribution System provides insufficient rations, forcing most North Koreans to supplement through informal markets or by engaging in subsistence production on private plots.

**Energy Crisis** — Chronic electricity shortages persist. Hydroelectric power generation is insufficient, coal production is declining, and the country lacks reliable access to petroleum. Blackouts are endemic; much of North Korea outside Pyongyang has electricity only a few hours daily.

**Infrastructure Decay** — Roads, railways, ports, and industrial facilities have deteriorated severely due to lack of maintenance and investment. The road network is minimal; railways (built during the colonial and Soviet eras) are the primary transport for heavy freight, but tracks and rolling stock are aging and unreliable.

## Economic Structure and Organization

**State Ownership** — All significant enterprises, land, and resources are nominally owned by the state. In practice, senior party officials and military commanders exercise control and capture much economic benefit through selective access to goods and services.

**Public Distribution System (PDS)** — The state attempts to provide basic rations of staple grains (rice, corn) and small amounts of other foods to registered populations. The system provides far below nutritional requirements (100–150 grams of grain per day per person, or about 800–1,200 calories) and is unreliable. Most North Koreans rely on supplementary income through informal work or market trading.

**Songbun System** — Access to economic goods is stratified by songbun (social class). The highest tier (party elites, military officers, scientists involved in nuclear/missile programs) enjoys comfortable lives; the core working class has modest access; the lower tiers (religious believers, capitalists' descendants, perceived anti-communist sympathizers) face restrictions and deprivation.

**Military Production** — The Strategic Rocket Force, the nuclear weapons program, and the Korean People's Army command disproportionate economic resources. Military spending is estimated at 15–25 percent of GDP, an extraordinarily high burden for a poor country.

**Mining** — North Korea has mineral resources (coal, iron ore, rare earths, magnesite). Mining remains a significant economic sector, primarily for domestic consumption and limited export. Work conditions in mines are notoriously harsh, and miners are often assigned through state conscription.

**Light Industry and Manufacturing** — Light manufacturing (textiles, chemicals, processed foods) is limited by lack of raw materials and energy. Most factories operate below capacity or are idle.

## International Economic Relations

**China** — North Korea's primary trading partner and economic lifeline. China provides food aid, fuel oil, and limited investment. The relationship is transactional and subject to Chinese political calculations.

**South Korea** — Periodic economic engagement under the "Sunshine Policy" (1998–2008) expanded trade and investment. The **Kaesong Industrial Complex** (개성공업지구), opened in 2004, was the most significant project: a special industrial zone just inside the North Korean border, staffed by approximately 55,000 North Korean workers employed by 125 South Korean companies, primarily in light manufacturing (garments, electronics, footwear). The zone generated approximately $100 million per year in wages (paid to the North Korean state, which passed a fraction to workers) and provided Pyongyang with a reliable hard-currency revenue stream. The complex was suspended by South Korea in February 2016 following North Korea's fourth nuclear test, denying Pyongyang an estimated $560 million in annual economic activity. Family reunions reunite separated families; food aid has been provided intermittently, suspended during periods of heightened tension, particularly after the 2010 Cheonan sinking and Yeonpyeong bombardment.

**United States** — Primarily antagonistic, though the US has provided food aid during famines. US sanctions restrict trade and financial relationships.

**International Organizations** — The World Food Programme, UNICEF, and other UN agencies operate limited programs in North Korea, providing humanitarian assistance but with highly restricted access and monitoring.

## Legacy and Prospects

The famine of the 1990s is a watershed in North Korean history, a catastrophe that revealed the regime's structural failures and the state's prioritization of military security over civilian welfare. The economy has never recovered to pre-collapse levels and remains fundamentally unstable and non-productive. International isolation, sanctions, and internal mismanagement ensure that structural change is unlikely without fundamental political transformation.
