# Energy Crisis and Electricity Shortage

**Category**: Economy  
**Key figures**: Soviet energy suppliers; KEDO negotiators; energy sector engineers; international observers

## Summary

North Korea faced a severe and prolonged energy crisis beginning in the early 1990s, triggered by the collapse of Soviet support and the loss of cheap Soviet oil and coal that had powered the planned economy since the DPRK's founding. The regime had built the country's economy on Soviet energy subsidies and trade relationships; when the USSR dissolved in 1991 and the Russian Federation withdrew preferential trade terms, the DPRK lost its primary source of petroleum, fuel oil, and coking coal. China, the successor energy supplier, provided aid at far lower volumes and at international market prices, creating an immediate and devastating gap.

The energy crisis directly precipitated the [famine of 1994–1998](economic-system-and-famine.md) (the "Arduous March"). Industrial production collapsed without fuel for factories and heating oil for homes and institutions. Agriculture suffered as fertilizer production — energy-intensive, centered on chemical plants like Vinalon factories in [Hamhung](../geography/hamhung.md) — stopped and irrigation pumps ceased functioning. Chronic electricity shortage persists into the present day, shaping every aspect of North Korean life and constraining the regime's economic options.

## Soviet Energy Dependency, 1945–1990

The DPRK's energy infrastructure was built during the Soviet period (1945–1991) on the assumption of continuing Soviet subsidy. The USSR provided oil and petroleum products at "friendship prices" — deeply discounted from world-market rates — under bilateral trade agreements renewed through the 1980s. Estimates suggest that Soviet oil exports to the DPRK reached approximately 500,000 tons per year by the late 1980s; combined with coal deliveries and technical assistance for power-plant construction and maintenance, this supply underwrote the entire centralized planned economy.

North Korea's own energy infrastructure had two pillars: hydropower and coal. The colonial-era Japanese (1910–1945) built large hydroelectric dams in the north — including the Suiho Dam (Supung) on the Yalu River, which at completion in 1943 was Asia's largest hydroelectric facility with a capacity of approximately 700 MW — exploiting the mountainous topography and rivers of the northern peninsula. These hydropower assets provided the base load. Soviet technical assistance expanded and modernized the grid after 1945, connecting industrial centers in [Hamhung](../geography/hamhung.md), Chongjin, and Wonsan to power-generation facilities. Thermal power plants burning Soviet-supplied coal and fuel oil supplemented hydropower during low-water seasons.

The total installed electrical generating capacity reached an estimated 7.5–8 GW by the late 1980s, sufficient on paper to power the industrial economy. However, the system was chronically underperforming even before the 1991 rupture: the Soviet-style planned economy invested in generation capacity without maintaining transmission infrastructure, and the grid suffered from endemic losses, outages, and equipment failures. By the late 1980s, actual output was probably 40–50% of installed capacity — a structural fragility that became catastrophic once Soviet oil flows stopped.

## The 1991 Rupture and Cascade Collapse

The USSR's dissolution in December 1991 and the Russian Federation's decision to trade at world-market prices eliminated the DPRK's preferential energy supply overnight. Soviet/Russian oil exports to the DPRK fell from approximately 500,000 tons per year in the late 1980s to roughly 40,000 tons by 1993 — a decline of over 90% in two years. China partially filled the gap but at market prices and far lower volumes than the Soviet supply.

The immediate effect was industrial paralysis. Factories designed to run on cheap Soviet oil ran out of fuel; thermal power plants could not obtain coal and fuel oil at sustainable prices; transport infrastructure — buses, trucks, tractors, locomotives — seized up for lack of diesel. The electrical grid, already fragile, deteriorated further as maintenance became impossible without spare parts and fuel. Total electricity generation, which may have reached 27–30 billion kWh per year in the late 1980s, collapsed to an estimated 10–12 billion kWh by the mid-1990s — less than half peak output.

Agriculture collapsed in direct consequence. North Korean agriculture was mechanized and chemical-intensive by design: irrigation pumps depended on electricity, tractors on diesel, and fertilizer production (the Hamhung chemical complex was the country's primary nitrogen-fertilizer manufacturer) on oil and electricity. When energy failed, irrigation stopped, tractors went unused, and fertilizer production halted. Crop yields fell by roughly 40–50% between 1992 and 1995. Combined with the 1994 floods and the 1995–1996 floods and droughts, the energy collapse triggered the [famine that killed an estimated 600,000–1,000,000 people](economic-system-and-famine.md) between 1994 and 1998.

## Hydropower and Coal: The Attempted Response

Facing the oil cutoff, the regime pivoted to domestic energy sources — primarily hydropower and coal. Neither could substitute adequately for Soviet oil, but both received heavy investment throughout the 1990s and 2000s.

Hydropower expansion became a state priority. Kim Jong-il declared hydroelectric dam construction a patriotic campaign, and the [Korean People's Army](../government-politics/korean-peoples-army.md) was mobilized for construction work. Major projects during this period included the Huichon Power Station (begun under Kim Jong-il, completed in phases under Kim Jong-un with the first generator coming online in 2012, nominal capacity ~300 MW), and the Paektusan Hero Youth Power Station. However, the DPRK's rivers are seasonal and subject to drought; hydropower output is highly variable, and the projects often remained substantially below designed capacity due to poor construction quality, lack of materials, and insufficient maintenance. Deforestation — widespread as citizens stripped hillsides for firewood during the famine — worsened watershed conditions, increasing silting of reservoirs and reducing effective storage.

Coal [mining](mining-and-resources.md) expanded as the primary domestic energy source for thermal power plants. Coal-burning plants at Bukchang, Pyongsong, and other locations became central to the electricity supply. But coal mining itself required electricity (for ventilation, pumping, and haulage), diesel (for heavy equipment), and steel (for infrastructure), creating circular dependencies that constrained expansion. Production volumes fluctuated with the overall economic crisis; coal quality degraded as accessible seams were exhausted and deeper extraction became technically and financially impossible without external inputs.

## The Agreed Framework and the KEDO Light-Water Reactor Project

The regime's nuclear weapons program and its energy crisis intersected in the 1994 Agreed Framework, negotiated between the United States and the DPRK under President Bill Clinton and signed in October 1994. The agreement was designed to freeze North Korea's plutonium-production capability at Yongbyon in exchange for:

- Two light-water reactors (LWRs) of approximately 1,000 MW each, to be built at a DPRK site on the east coast
- Annual deliveries of 500,000 tons of heavy fuel oil (HFO) to compensate the DPRK for the electricity it would forego while its existing reactor infrastructure was frozen
- A pathway to normalized relations

The Korean Peninsula Energy Development Organization (KEDO), a multilateral consortium involving the United States, South Korea, Japan, and the European Union, was established to implement the framework. KEDO selected a site at Kumho (near Sinpo in South Hamgyong Province) for the LWR construction and delivered approximately 500,000 tons of HFO annually from 1995 until the framework's collapse. The reactors were never completed: construction began in 2000 but the Bush administration suspended HFO deliveries in late 2002 following the disclosure of a covert uranium-enrichment program, the DPRK withdrew from the NPT in January 2003, and KEDO suspended construction in December 2003. KEDO's Executive Board formally terminated the light-water reactor project in 2006, and the partially built structures at Kumho were abandoned.

The failed KEDO project illustrates how completely the energy crisis intertwined with the nuclear issue. The DPRK's public rationale for its nuclear program included the claim that it needed nuclear power to address the electricity shortage; in practice, the nuclear weapons program diverted resources from civilian energy while creating the diplomatic conflict that prevented outside assistance.

## Present-Day Power Deprivation

Satellite nighttime imagery from NASA and other sources shows the Korean Peninsula at night: South Korea blazes with light; North Korea is almost entirely dark, a void between the illuminated south and the Chinese coast. Pyongyang has scattered light; the rest of the country is nearly invisible. These images are among the most striking visual representations of the DPRK's electricity crisis.

In practical terms, most North Koreans outside Pyongyang receive electricity for as little as two to four hours per day, often unpredictably. In many rural and provincial areas, electricity may be unavailable for weeks or months at a time. Blackouts are structural rather than emergency: the grid is simply insufficient to meet demand. Citizens who can afford it purchase small generators, car batteries for adapters, or LED lighting powered by small solar panels — a grey-market energy economy that has grown since the 2000s.

The regime allocates scarce electricity through a strict priority hierarchy:

1. **Military and security facilities** — [KPA](../government-politics/korean-peoples-army.md) bases, [Bowibu](../government-politics/state-security-department.md) installations, and nuclear/missile research sites receive uninterrupted power
2. **[Pyongyang](../geography/pyongyang.md)** — the capital receives priority over all other civilian users, reinforcing the political geography of privilege
3. **State enterprises and key industries** — selected factories and mining operations receive allocations
4. **Provincial cities and towns** — receive reduced and intermittent supply
5. **Rural areas** — receive minimal or no reliable supply

Hospitals in provincial cities operate on generator backup when electricity fails; emergency surgeries have been conducted by candlelight. Schools hold classes in unheated buildings during winter because electricity for heating is unavailable. The energy shortage is thus directly life-threatening, contributing to excess mortality through hypothermia, medical equipment failures, and food storage problems year-round.

## Sanctions, the COVID Border Closure, and Post-2017 Constraints

International sanctions, particularly the UN Security Council resolutions of 2017 (UNSCR 2371, 2375, 2397) adopted in response to the DPRK's nuclear tests and Hwasong ICBM launches, targeted energy supply alongside coal exports. UNSCR 2397 (December 2017) capped petroleum product exports to the DPRK at 500,000 barrels per year and crude oil at 4 million barrels per year — far below the DPRK's needs. Although China and Russia have not fully enforced these caps and smuggling through ship-to-ship transfers has continued (see [foreign trade and sanctions](foreign-trade-and-sanctions.md)), the constraints have further squeezed the DPRK's access to oil.

The COVID-19 border closure (February 2020 – late 2023) compounded energy shortages by stopping most cross-border trade, including informal coal smuggling and petroleum deliveries. The closure coincided with a period of unusually poor harvests and severe flooding (2020–2021), creating what Kim Jong-un himself acknowledged publicly was the country's worst economic crisis since the Arduous March.

## Significance

The energy crisis was the material foundation of the famine and economic collapse of the 1990s. Without Soviet energy, the centrally planned economy could not function, revealing how dependent the regime had become on external subsidy — a direct contradiction of [juche's](../culture-society/state-ideology-and-propaganda.md) proclaimed self-reliance. The crisis also deepened the regime's strategic incentives for nuclear weapons development: nuclear weapons became, in effect, a substitute for the economic development the regime could not deliver. The chronic lack of electricity continues to shape everyday life and remains largely unsolved after more than three decades of management.
