# Religion and Atheism in North Korea

**Category**: Culture & Society  
**Key figures**: Kim Il-sung, Kim Jong-il, Kim Jong-un; Christian pastors and believers; international religious organizations

## Summary

North Korea maintains an official state ideology of atheism and materialism, inherited from Soviet Marxist-Leninist doctrine adopted during the founding period. The regime treats organized religion as a direct threat to totalitarian control, particularly Christianity, which historically had deep roots in Korea — especially in the north, where Korean Protestantism originated and thrived before 1945. The regime systematized the suppression of all religious practice through education, propaganda, surveillance, and the [kwanliso political prison system](kwanliso.md). Religious believers — Christians in particular — face imprisonment, torture, and execution classified as "hostile class" crimes.

The regime permits only state-controlled religious organizations for propaganda purposes: a state-controlled Protestant church, a Catholic church, and a Buddhist temple in Pyongyang, all staffed with regime appointees and used to create the appearance of religious tolerance for international audiences. These institutions hold occasional services attended by foreigners but function as museums and propaganda tools rather than genuine houses of worship. Outside Pyongyang, organized religious practice is virtually nonexistent and punishable as treason.

Christianity's historical prevalence in northern Korea — where missionary activity concentrated from the late 1800s onward — made it a particular target. The regime views Christianity as incompatible with the absolute loyalty demanded by the personality cult and the [juche ideology's](state-ideology-and-propaganda.md) materialist worldview. Believers who attempt to worship privately, listen to Christian radio broadcasts from the South, or possess Bibles face immediate arrest and are typically sent to kwanliso camps. The regime classifies Christianity as a Western imperialist infiltration and a form of ideological subversion.

## Historical Background: Christianity in Northern Korea

Before the DPRK's founding in 1948, northern Korea was the heartland of Korean Christianity. Protestant missionaries — particularly American Presbyterians and Methodists — arrived in the late nineteenth century and concentrated their work in Pyongyang and the surrounding region. By the early twentieth century, Pyongyang had earned the informal title "Jerusalem of the East": the city and its surrounding region hosted a dense concentration of churches and a vibrant Christian community that included an educated, activist elite. The 1907 Pyongyang Revival, a mass awakening that spread through Korean Protestant congregations, was one of the most dramatic religious events in modern East Asian history and permanently anchored Protestantism in the north.

The Korean YMCA, founded in Seoul in 1903 and with a strong Pyongyang branch, exemplified how Christianity fused with Korean nationalism during the Japanese colonial period. Christian leaders played a central role in the 1 March 1919 independence movement (March 1st Movement), when 33 Korean representatives — the majority of them Christians — signed the Declaration of Independence and organized nationwide demonstrations. Japanese colonial authorities suppressed the movement violently, but Christianity's association with anti-colonial nationalism deepened its hold in the north.

Kim Il-sung's own family had Christian connections: his grandfather was a Protestant deacon, and Kim himself attended church as a child in Mangyongdae (Pyongyang's western suburb) before turning to communist organizing in Manchuria. This family history gave the regime both intimate knowledge of Christianity's popular roots and ideological motivation to destroy what it understood as a rival loyalty structure.

By 1945, when the Soviet occupation began in the north, an estimated 200,000–300,000 Christians lived in northern Korea. The Soviet Military Administration initially tolerated Christian institutions while organizing a parallel political structure. Kim Il-sung's consolidation of power from 1946 onward progressively dismantled Christian organizations: churches were nationalized, Christian political parties dissolved, and clergy who refused collaboration were arrested or fled south. By the outbreak of the Korean War in 1950, the organized church in the north had been effectively destroyed. The mass southward flight of Korean civilians during the war — including the November–December 1950 Hungnam evacuation, in which approximately 98,000 civilians escaped to the south — included a large proportion of northern Christians. Those who remained faced systematic persecution.

## Soviet Atheism and the Marxist-Leninist Framework

The DPRK's official atheism derives directly from Soviet Marxism-Leninism, which treated religion as a reactionary class ideology — in Marx's phrase, "the opium of the people" — that diverted the working class from revolutionary consciousness and served ruling-class interests. The Soviet occupying administration after 1945 brought this framework wholesale into northern Korea, and Kim Il-sung adopted it as regime doctrine in the DPRK's founding constitution (1948), which guaranteed freedom of religious belief as a formal right while structurally prohibiting organized practice inconsistent with socialist construction.

The contradiction between formal constitutional guarantee and operational suppression is characteristic of the DPRK's legal system. The state acknowledges a nominal right to belief but treats organized worship, proselytism, and any cross-border religious connection as criminal. The [Workers' Party of Korea](../government-politics/workers-party-of-korea.md) oversees ideological conformity, and the [State Security Department (Bowibu)](../government-politics/state-security-department.md) enforces it through a network of informants, mandatory self-criticism sessions, and the inminban neighborhood-surveillance system documented in the [state ideology and propaganda](state-ideology-and-propaganda.md) file.

## State-Controlled Religious Institutions

The regime maintains a small number of state-controlled religious institutions in Pyongyang, operated by the Korean Christian Federation (Protestant), the Korean Catholic Association, the Korean Buddhists' Federation, and the Korean Chondogyo Central Guidance Committee (for the indigenous Korean Chondogyo religion). These bodies present North Korea as a country with religious freedom to foreign audiences — particularly to delegations from Western churches and international religious organizations visiting Pyongyang.

The two most prominent showpieces are:

- **Bongsu Church** (봉수교회), a Protestant church opened in 1988 and used to host foreign delegations and ecumenical visitors. Services are conducted in Korean, but attendance is controlled and congregation members are understood to be regime-approved functionaries rather than genuine believers.
- **Changchung Cathedral** (장충성당), a Catholic church also opened in 1988 and similarly used for diplomatic display. North Korea claims a small Catholic community, but there are no ordained priests — Mass cannot be validly celebrated — and the institution functions as a facade.

The 1988 openings coincided with South Korea hosting the Seoul Olympics and the DPRK's international-image campaign of that period. Pyongyang's Kwangbop Buddhist Temple, a restored Koryo-era structure, similarly receives foreign Buddhist delegations. Outside Pyongyang, no equivalent institutions exist, and foreign access is prohibited. The [education system](education-system.md) explicitly teaches children that religion is a superstition incompatible with scientific materialism and devotion to the Supreme Leader.

## Juche as a Quasi-Religious Substitute

North Korea's [state ideology of juche](state-ideology-and-propaganda.md) performs many functions of organized religion while formally repudiating theism. The Supreme Leader is presented as an eternal, omniscient, and morally perfect figure whose guidance provides the meaning, purpose, and comfort that religion supplies in other societies. The three-generation personality cult frames Kim Il-sung as an immortal presence (his formal title is "Eternal President"), Kim Jong-il as the embodiment of juche's ideological perfection, and Kim Jong-un as the legitimate heir of a dynastic mission. Citizens participate in mandatory rituals — bowing to portraits and statues, attendance at mass mobilization events, public self-criticism sessions — that parallel religious observance.

Defector testimony consistently describes the personality cult as functionally religious for citizens who have never known an alternative. The cult provides an origin story (the Kim family's mythologized guerrilla struggle and the Paektu birthplace narrative — see [Mount Paektu](../geography/mount-paektu.md)), an eschatology (the future reunification of Korea under DPRK leadership), and a system of grace and damnation (the [songbun class classification](state-ideology-and-propaganda.md) that determines life outcomes). This quasi-religious architecture reinforces the regime's hostility to Christianity: a rival system of transcendence, community, and authority that could compete with the cult's monopoly on meaning.

## Persecution: Classification and Punishment

Religious believers — above all Christians — are classified in the DPRK's political taxonomy as "hostile class" elements, assigned the lowest songbun grades, and targeted for the most severe punishment under the regime's laws. The 2009 revision of the Criminal Law (expanded from 2012 onward) treats religious activity without state authorization as a serious crime against the state. Specific offenses include:

- Possession of a Bible or religious literature (punishable by imprisonment or execution)
- Listening to South Korean Christian radio broadcasts (Radio Free Asia, Far East Broadcasting Company, Voice of America — which have broadcast Korean-language Christian programming since the 1950s)
- Proselytism or organizing any unauthorized religious gathering
- Contact with foreign missionaries or religious organizations operating across the Chinese border

The Bowibu maintains specialized units focused on detecting Christianity, monitoring the Chinese border for smuggled Bibles and religious materials, and surveilling communities in North Hamgyong and Ryanggang provinces (which border China and have the most exposure to cross-border contact). Informants within the inminban structure are trained to report religious activity by neighbors and family members.

Punishment for detected Christianity typically results in arrest by the Bowibu and transfer to a [kwanliso political prison camp](kwanliso.md), where the individual and often their entire family are interned under the doctrine of collective punishment (yonzwa — guilt by association extending to three generations). Survivor accounts from kwanliso inmates, including Kang Cheol-hwan's *Aquariums of Pyongyang* (2000 French edition; 2001 English edition), describe Christian prisoners as a distinct category of inmates receiving especially harsh treatment. North Korean defectors who crossed into China and encountered Christian missionaries face death or long-term kwanliso sentences upon repatriation.

## International Documentation and Response

The 2014 UN Commission of Inquiry on Human Rights in the DPRK, chaired by Australian jurist Michael Kirby and informed by public hearings in Seoul, London, and Washington, D.C., concluded that religious persecution constitutes crimes against humanity as defined under the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court. The Commission's 400-page report documented systematic extermination, imprisonment, torture, and enforced disappearance of Christians and members of other religious groups.

Human rights organizations — Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, the Committee for Human Rights in North Korea (HRNK), and Open Doors — maintain ongoing documentation of North Korea's status as one of the world's most severe persecutors of Christians. Open Doors has ranked North Korea at or near the top of its World Watch List of countries that most severely persecute Christians every year since the list's inception.

The international community has been unable to compel change because the DPRK's diplomatic isolation and military deterrent (see [nuclear weapons program](../history/nuclear-weapons-program.md)) insulate it from external pressure. The regime dismisses all documentation as fabrication and anti-DPRK propaganda.

## Significance

Religious suppression is central to the DPRK's totalitarian apparatus. By eliminating alternative sources of meaning, community, and authority, the regime consolidated its monopoly over every aspect of citizens' lives. The official atheism doctrine reinforces the personality cult by positioning the Supreme Leader as the sole source of truth, guidance, and salvation — a quasi-religious function performed by a political leader. This ideological foundation underpins the [education system](education-system.md), state propaganda, the inminban surveillance networks, and the [kwanliso system](kwanliso.md) itself.

The persecution of Christians also reflects the regime's broader fear of international connection and ideological contamination. Christian networks historically crossed borders and created loyalties outside state control — an intolerable threat to a state that demands absolute loyalty and monopolizes all information. The treatment of Christians in kwanliso camps, documented by survivors, reveals religious persecution as one of the regime's core mechanisms of terror. For the international community, North Korea's religious suppression is a human-rights issue and a window into the regime's ideological absolutism.
