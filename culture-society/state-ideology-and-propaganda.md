# State Ideology and Propaganda

**Category**: Culture & Society  
**Key figures**: [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md), [Kim Jong-il](../people/kim-jong-il.md), [Kim Jong-un](../people/kim-jong-un.md)

## Summary

The Democratic People's Republic of Korea is fundamentally a propaganda state. The regime's control of narrative, ideology, and information is as central to governance as military force or political repression—arguably more so, since it creates the conditions under which repression is accepted or invisible to those subject to it. At the core of the system is **juche** (주체, self-reliance), the state ideology formulated by [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md) that positions North Korea as an independent actor pursuing its own historical destiny, while in practice justifying the absolute concentration of power in the Kim family. This ideology is disseminated through every institution: the state media monopoly, mandatory mass education beginning in infancy, compulsory participation in mass rallies and festivals, and the omnipresent personality cult surrounding each successive Supreme Leader.

## Juche: Origins and Content

The term *juche* was first used publicly by Kim Il-sung in a **December 28, 1955** speech, "On Eliminating Dogmatism and Formalism and Establishing Juche in Ideological Work," in which he criticized Korean Workers' Party cadres for blindly imitating Soviet and Chinese communist practices rather than developing Korea-specific approaches. The speech was a political maneuver within an intra-party struggle as much as an ideological statement; juche was not systematically elaborated as a formal philosophy until the 1970s.

Juche doctrine holds that humans are the masters of their own destiny, that a nation must be independent in ideology (chajusong), economy (jarip), and military affairs (chawi), and that the individual's role is to subordinate themselves to the collective under the guidance of the Supreme Leader. In practice this translates to:

- **Political independence** from both Soviet and Chinese tutelage (an important claim given that North Korea was in fact installed by Soviet occupation forces in 1945 and dependent on Chinese intervention to survive the [Korean War](../history/korean-war.md))
- **Economic self-reliance**, used to justify autarky and resistance to international economic integration
- **Military self-defense** as justification for the nuclear weapons program
- **Absolute deference** to the Supreme Leader, whose wisdom is said to embody the collective will of the Korean people

Kim Jong-il extensively developed juche theory in the 1970s–80s, publishing works such as *On the Juche Idea* (1982). He also introduced the supplementary concept of **Kimilsungism** to specify that juche's authentic embodiment was in Kim Il-sung's own thought and leadership, creating a circular reinforcement between ideology and personality cult.

## The Personality Cult

North Korea's personality cult is among the most elaborate in documented history, rivaling or exceeding those of Stalin's Soviet Union and Mao's China in its pervasiveness and mythological ambition:

**Kim Il-sung (Supreme Leader, 1948–1994)**  
Born April 15, 1912 (a date so significant it became the basis for the DPRK's alternative calendar system, the **Juche calendar**, counting years from his birth as Year 1). Kim Il-sung is styled the "Eternal President of the Republic"—a title that persisted after his death in 1994, meaning North Korea is constitutionally governed by a dead man. His mythology includes claims of supernatural powers, miraculous feats of military leadership, and divine-like omniscience. More than 34,000 statues of Kim Il-sung exist throughout the country. Every adult North Korean is required to wear a **Kim Il-sung badge** (lapel pin with his image) at all times in public.

**Kim Jong-il (Dear Leader, 1994–2011)**  
The mythology surrounding [Kim Jong-il](../people/kim-jong-il.md) centers on his claimed birth at [Mount Paektu](../geography/mount-paektu.md) on February 16, 1942 (a date propagandized as so momentous that a double rainbow appeared over the mountain and a new star was born). His birthday, February 16, became a state holiday called the **Day of the Shining Star** (광명성절). He is credited with writing more than 1,500 books during his time at Kim Il-sung University and with being able to compose an opera in under a week. Like his father, he wears the lapel pin, and after his death in December 2011 he was designated "Eternal General Secretary of the Korean Workers' Party."

**Kim Jong-un (Supreme Leader, 2011–present)**  
[Kim Jong-un](../people/kim-jong-un.md)'s personality cult builds on both predecessors but adapts for a changed media environment. The phrase **"Paektu bloodline"** (백두혈통) explicitly frames his rulership as genetic inheritance from the sacred mountain and revolutionary lineage. He has been photographed riding horses at [Mount Paektu](../geography/mount-paektu.md), a deliberate invocation of the heroic imagery associated with his grandfather. Unlike his father, Kim Jong-un appears on live state television and gives public speeches—a stylistic modernization of cult presentation while maintaining its ideological content.

## Propaganda Apparatus

### State Media

All media in North Korea is state-owned and operates under direct party supervision. The primary outlets are:

- **Korean Central News Agency (KCNA)**, the sole domestic wire service, founded 1946, providing all authorized news content
- **Korean Central Television (KCTV)**, the primary broadcast channel, operating since 1963, with programming consisting of political documentaries, approved dramas, and news
- **Rodong Sinmun** (Workers' Daily), the Korean Workers' Party newspaper, founded 1945; its front-page content effectively announces state policy
- **Radio** (Korean Central Broadcasting Station) reaches rural and non-electrified areas, and radios are hardwired to receive only state frequencies

Foreign broadcasts are illegal to receive. Shortwave radios require modification by a state technician to remove all frequencies except state ones. Possession of unauthorized media—South Korean dramas, foreign films—is punishable by labor camp imprisonment; the crime is classified as a form of ideological treason. Despite this, a significant informal market for smuggled USB drives containing foreign content has developed, particularly since the mid-2000s.

### Mass Mobilization and Rituals

The regime maintains obligatory participation in ideological rituals throughout life:

- **Mandatory "study sessions"** for all workers and residents: weekly meetings (typically 2 hours) at worksites or residential units where party texts are read, personal ideology is self-examined, and neighbors report on each other's political reliability
- **Mass games (Arirang/Mass Performance)**: mass synchronized gymnastic and artistic performances involving tens of thousands of participants that were conducted approximately annually from 2002–2019, showcasing collective discipline and ideological themes. The Arirang festival was listed in the Guinness World Records as the largest choreographed performance, involving up to 100,000 performers
- **Mandatory mourning**: After the deaths of Kim Il-sung (July 8, 1994) and Kim Jong-il (December 17, 2011), nationwide mandatory mourning periods of 10 and 3 days respectively were imposed, with reports that insufficient public displays of grief could result in punishment

### Cultural Production

All cultural production—literature, film, music, theater, painting—must serve political goals and embody approved themes. The **April 25 Film Studio** in Pyongyang produces films under direct party supervision. Kim Jong-il personally supervised North Korean cinema as a passionate cinephile; he famously orchestrated the 1978 kidnapping of South Korean director Shin Sang-ok and his actress wife Choi Eun-hee to improve North Korean film production quality. The two were held for eight years before escaping in Vienna in 1986. North Korean films typically feature themes of wartime heroism against American imperialists, sacrifice for the collective, and the transformative wisdom of the Supreme Leader.

The **Korean People's Army Merited Chorus** and other state ensembles perform approved musical works. Under Kim Jong-un, the formation of the **Moranbong Band** (2012), an all-female pop group performing songs with nationalist and ideological themes in contemporary pop arrangements, marked an aestheticization of propaganda for a younger domestic audience.

## The Songbun System

The **songbun** (성분, "background/class status") system categorizes all citizens into hereditary political classes based on family background and demonstrated loyalty, determining access to education, employment, housing, rations, and privileges. It was formalized through a series of nationwide loyalty investigations in the late 1950s and 1960s, which classified all families according to their activities during the Japanese colonial period and the Korean War. Citizens were sorted into three broad categories:

- **Core class** (핵심계층): families of revolutionaries, workers, and those who supported the regime—roughly 28% of the population. Eligible for the best positions, Pyongyang residency, and university education
- **Wavering class** (동요계층): ordinary workers and peasants without distinguished revolutionary or counter-revolutionary backgrounds—roughly 45% of the population
- **Hostile class** (적대계층): families with connections to South Korea, Japan, religious practice, or prior landowner/merchant status—roughly 27% of the population. Subject to restriction from Pyongyang, elite education, and party membership

Songbun status is inherited and very difficult to change, though it can theoretically be improved through military service or party achievements, or degraded through crimes, defection, or family members' misconduct. The system is tightly integrated with the propaganda apparatus: citizens in the "hostile" class are ideologically suspect by definition, requiring greater surveillance.

## Information Control and Surveillance

The Ministry of **State Security** (Bowibu, 보위부), North Korea's secret police, operates an extensive surveillance apparatus extending to neighborhood-level **inminban** (people's unit) groups of approximately 20–40 households. Each inminban has an appointed leader (typically a woman) who monitors residents' activities, schedules, visitors, and adherence to ideological requirements, reporting to party authorities. This creates a pervasive panopticon in which neighbors surveill neighbors.

Citizens cannot travel between cities without official permits. Pyongyang residency is a privilege reserved for songbun elites. Movement to border areas requires additional permits.

## Propaganda and the Economy

The connection between ideology and economic management is explicit in North Korea. The **Chollima Movement** (千里馬, "thousand-li horse"), launched by Kim Il-sung in 1956, modeled on the Soviet Stakhanovite movement, urged workers to superhuman productive efforts through revolutionary spirit. The movement's name references a mythological flying horse that symbolized speed and aspiration. Subsequent "speed campaigns"—including the **Chollima Work Team Movement** (1959), **Pyongyang Speed** (1970s), and **Masikryong Speed** (2010s, named after the Masikryong ski resort built by Kim Jong-un)—repeatedly invoked the same mobilization framework, implicitly acknowledging through propaganda what the regime denied in official statistics: that normal economic processes were failing to meet targets.

The [economic system's](../economy/economic-system-and-famine.md) failures during the 1990s famine (the "Arduous March") presented a severe test for the propaganda apparatus. The famine, which killed an estimated 240,000–3,500,000 people between 1994 and 1998, was never acknowledged as such by state media, which attributed food shortages entirely to international sanctions and natural disasters, denying any connection to the command economy's structural failures or the regime's prioritization of military spending.

## Adaptation Under Kim Jong-un

The propaganda apparatus has shown considerable adaptability under Kim Jong-un. The emergence of smuggled USB drives and DVDs containing South Korean and foreign content has produced a situation in which a significant minority of North Koreans, particularly in border areas and urban centers, have access to external information while maintaining surface-level compliance. The regime's response has included both intensified enforcement campaigns and partial acknowledgment that ideological insularity cannot be absolute.

Kim Jong-un's personal image management—appearing publicly with his wife Ri Sol-ju, building ski resorts and water parks, tolerating elements of consumer culture in Pyongyang—represents an attempt to supplement revolutionary legitimacy with developmental legitimacy, presenting the regime as providing modernity rather than merely demanding sacrifice. This adaptation does not constitute liberalization; the kwan-li-so prison camps continue to operate, and the fundamental structure of information control remains intact.
