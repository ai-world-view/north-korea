# Hamhung

**Hamhung** (함흥) is the second-largest city in the DPRK, located on the east coast in South Hamgyong Province (함경남도, Hamgyŏngnam-do). With an estimated population of 700,000–800,000 — official DPRK figures are unreliable and have not been independently verified since the 1993 census — it is the primary industrial center of the country outside Pyongyang and serves as a vital hub for chemical, textile, and military production. Historically a port city of merchant culture, Hamhung was transformed under communist rule into a socialist industrial zone; it bears the scars of war, sanctions, and economic collapse alongside relics of its pre-modern and colonial past.

## Geography and Location

Hamhung sits at approximately 39.9°N, 127.5°E, on the Songchon River (성천강, Sŏngch'ŏn-gang) as it flows toward Korea Bay on the Sea of Japan (East Sea). The city lies approximately 280 kilometers northeast of Pyongyang by rail and is the administrative capital of South Hamgyong Province. The terrain is coastal plain backed by the Hamgyong mountain range; the city occupies a strategic position on the historic trade route between the capital and the northeastern border regions near Russia and China.

The port district of **Hungnam** (흥남) — formally a separate city but now effectively merged with Hamhung as its eastern industrial and port quarter — lies approximately 15 kilometers to the southeast on Korea Bay. Hungnam's port is ice-free year-round due to the warm Tsushima current, giving it potential maritime commercial value — though this potential is severely constrained by sanctions and regime policy.

Access by rail: the **Hamgyong Line** connects Hamhung northward to Chongjin (함경선) and southward toward Pyongyang via the Pyongbu Line. Rail travel between Hamhung and Pyongyang takes roughly 6–8 hours on a functioning passenger service — a journey that in practice can take much longer given fuel shortages and rolling stock failures.

## Pre-Modern and Colonial History

Hamhung has roots extending to the Three Kingdoms period. During the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392), the area was an administrative seat in the northeastern march territories. Under the Joseon dynasty, Hamhung served as the capital of Hamgyong Province and as a significant military base guarding against northern incursions.

**Yi Sŏnggye** — the founder of the Joseon dynasty who reigned as King Taejo — was born near Hamhung in 1335. His birthplace is commemorated in North Korean historical narratives; the regime treats this connection selectively, preferring to emphasize the revolutionary heritage of Kim Il-sung over pre-modern dynastic connections.

In the late 19th century, as Korea opened to foreign contact, Hamhung became a center of **Protestant Christian missionary activity**. American Presbyterian and Methodist missionaries established schools and churches from the 1890s onward; several hundred local families converted. This Christian legacy would later mark those families as ideologically suspect under communist rule — a shadow that persisted through songbun classification for generations.

The city's modern industrial character emerged during **Japanese colonial occupation (1910–1945)**. The Government-General of Korea developed Hamhung and neighboring Hungnam as a chemical-industrial complex, taking advantage of local coal, water, and proximity to the port. The **Hungnam Nitrogen Fertilizer Factory** (흥남질소비료공장) — later known as the Hungnam Fertilizer Complex — was begun in 1927 by the Japan Nitrogen Fertilizer Company (Chisso Corporation), with construction expanding through the 1930s. By 1945 it was one of Asia's largest chemical facilities, producing synthetic ammonia, ammonium sulfate fertilizers, and explosives precursors. The complex employed tens of thousands of Korean workers under harsh colonial labor conditions.

## The Korean War and the Hungnam Evacuation

The Korean War (1950–1953) produced one of Hamhung's most dramatic historical episodes: the **Hungnam Evacuation** of December 1950.

After United Nations (primarily US and South Korean) forces swept northward in autumn 1950 and approached the Chinese border, the People's Liberation Army of China intervened massively in late October–November 1950. The **Battle of Chosin Reservoir** (장진호 전투, November 27 – December 13, 1950) — fought in the mountains approximately 80 kilometers northwest of Hamhung — resulted in US Marine and Army forces fighting through encirclement by Chinese divisions in brutal winter conditions (temperatures reaching −30°C to −40°C). The Marines and soldiers reached Hamhung in early December after a fighting withdrawal.

The subsequent **Hungnam Evacuation (Operation Christmas Cargo)**, December 9–24, 1950, was one of the largest military evacuations in history: approximately 105,000 UN troops and roughly 98,000 North Korean civilian refugees were loaded onto 193 ships under the command of Rear Admiral James Doyle and evacuated to Busan and Geoje Island in South Korea before Chinese forces could encircle the port. The evacuation was completed on Christmas Eve 1950; ammunition dumps and port facilities were destroyed to prevent capture. Many of the civilian evacuees became permanent refugees in South Korea; their descendants are among the millions of divided families separated by the 38th parallel.

The war's bombing campaigns reduced much of Hamhung and Hungnam to rubble. Reconstruction, supported by Soviet and Chinese advisors and materiel, consumed the late 1950s and early 1960s.

## Vinalon and the Chemical Industry

Post-war Hamhung became associated with one of the DPRK's signature technological claims: **Vinalon** (비날론), a synthetic textile fiber. Vinalon is made from calcium carbide derived from coal and limestone — both available domestically — and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA). It was developed by Korean chemist **Ri Sung-gi** (리승기, 1905–1996), who had worked in Japan and defected to North Korea in 1950.

The February 8 Vinalon Complex (2·8비날론연합기업소), built in Hamhung in the 1950s–1960s, became a showcase of Juche self-sufficiency: a North Korean-invented fiber, produced from North Korean raw materials, substituting for cotton (scarce domestically) and nylon or polyester (requiring petroleum). Kim Il-sung promoted Vinalon as proof that the DPRK could solve its own problems without dependence on foreign imports. Vinalon clothes — rough in texture, poor at absorbing moisture, hot in summer — became ubiquitous in North Korean life through the 1960s–1980s.

The collapse of Hamhung's energy supply during the 1990s famine effectively shut down the Vinalon complex; the energy-intensive electrolysis process requires substantial and reliable electricity. The complex reportedly resumed partial production under Kim Jong-un but at reduced capacity.

The **Hungnam Fertilizer Complex** (흥남비료련합기업소) remained the largest facility in the Hamhung-Hungnam industrial zone, producing synthetic ammonia and ammonium nitrate (fertilizer and explosives precursor). Its output is essential both to North Korean agriculture (which depends on chemical fertilizers) and to ordnance production. International analysts estimate the complex's annual production capacity at roughly 200,000–400,000 metric tons of fertilizer when operating at full capacity; chronic fuel and electricity shortages typically reduce actual output far below this figure.

## Industry and Economy

Hamhung's economy is centered on heavy industry. Key sectors:

**Chemical production**: The Hungnam complex is the flagship; additional chemical plants produce solvents, plastics, and pharmaceutical precursors. Environmental degradation from chemical runoff and atmospheric pollution has been severe; the Songchon River and nearby coastal waters show contamination from decades of industrial discharge.

**Textiles**: Mills established during the colonial period — repurposed and nationalized after 1945 — produce fabrics for domestic use. During the Cold War, Hamhung textiles supplied Soviet allies and generated hard currency. Sanctions and the post-Soviet collapse curtailed exports; today production serves primarily domestic need and military uniform production.

**Military-industrial production**: Ordnance factories, ammunition plants, and weapons-assembly facilities operate in the Hamhung-Hungnam complex; the city is a key node in North Korea's defense-industrial network. Specific facilities include munitions plants producing artillery shells and rocket propellants. The military economic role gives Hamhung strategic importance disproportionate to its civilian economic output.

**Fisheries**: Korea Bay and the nearby coastal waters support fishing, though the fleet is small and poorly maintained. Fishing provides a rare source of protein for local populations.

## The Famine and Its Aftermath

The 1994–1998 famine struck Hamhung with devastating force. South Hamgyong Province was one of the hardest-hit regions in the country; local accounts and defector testimonies suggest that between 10–20% of the provincial population may have died of starvation or related illness during the Arduous March years.

Industrial collapse compounded food scarcity: factories stopped paying workers as the public distribution system (PDS) broke down. Workers who depended on factory distribution of food rations received nothing; without employment income or informal market access, many families faced total destitution. The population declined noticeably — exact figures are unknown but multiple indicators (school enrollment, housing density estimates from satellite imagery analysis by the Committee for Human Rights in North Korea) suggest a significant drop in the city's population during 1994–1998.

Recovery has been slow and geographically uneven. The **jangmadang** (장마당) — informal markets — emerged in Hamhung during the famine as survival mechanisms and have persisted. Hamhung has a relatively active market economy by North Korean standards, owing to its port access, industrial base, and geographic distance from the capital's tightest surveillance. Traders and small entrepreneurs operate in gray-market spaces that the regime tolerates intermittently.

## Urbanism and Contemporary Life

Hamhung's urban landscape reflects socialist planning principles overlaid on older Joseon-era and colonial street patterns. Wide boulevards named for revolutionary heroes — Kim Il-sung Square occupies the civic center — are flanked by 4–6 story concrete apartment blocks, most built in the 1960s–1980s. Many structures are now badly deteriorated: exposed rebar, cracked facades, stairwells without lighting.

The civic center features the requisite:
- **Grand monuments to Kim Il-sung** (and, after 2012 additions, Kim Jong-il): bronze statues on a raised plinth with permanent flower offerings
- **Hamhung Grand Theatre**: a large Soviet-modernist performance hall used for political rallies and approved cultural events
- **Mangyong Hill**: elevated public park with views of the city and coast, maintained as a regime prestige site

Some colonial-era Japanese architecture survives in commercial districts — older brick construction with different window proportions visible amid the concrete socialist blocks. Religious buildings that survived the Korean War were converted to warehouses, cultural halls, or demolished; the Christian churches built by missionaries in the 1890s–1930s no longer function as places of worship.

**Electricity**: Hamhung receives power primarily from the Pujŏn River hydroelectric system and the North Korean grid, both chronically insufficient. Outages lasting many hours per day are the norm; industrial facilities receive priority over residential neighborhoods.

**Transportation**: The city is served by trams (some operating since the 1940s) and diesel buses, both poorly maintained. Bicycle use is widespread. Private car ownership is extremely rare outside party and military elite.

The songbun class stratification manifests spatially: party cadres and military officers occupy better-maintained apartment buildings nearer the city center; politically unreliable families — those with Christian ancestry, relatives who fled to South Korea, or records of ideological transgression — are assigned the worst housing on the urban periphery.

## Cultural and Religious Suppression

Hamhung's Protestant Christian history is a suppressed legacy that continues to affect family fates through songbun classification. The missionary schools established in the 1890s–1930s produced generations of educated Koreans; many of their descendants fled south during the Korean War evacuation. Those who remained carry the stigma of "Christian-background" families in their hereditary class standing.

The Korean Christian Federation operates a nominal presence in Hamhung — a regime-approved "church" that functions as a propaganda prop for foreign visitors asserting religious freedom. Authentic religious practice is forbidden; punishment for unauthorized worship can include imprisonment in the political camp system.

Traditional Korean culture (music, dance, literature) survives in state-sanctioned forms: revolutionary operas, mass games choreography, and propaganda films produced at the Hamhung Film Studio. Independent cultural expression is not permitted.

## Port and External Contact

The port of Hungnam handles domestic cargo — grain, coal, industrial supplies — and limited regional maritime commerce. North Korean vessels are few and poorly maintained; international shipping into Hungnam is restricted by UN Security Council sanctions (Resolutions 2270, 2321, 2375 adopted 2016–2017 in response to nuclear tests) that limit North Korean port access and prohibit shipments of luxury goods and weapons-relevant materials.

Coastal and submarine naval installations in the vicinity of Hamhung-Hungnam give the city strategic military significance. The DPRK's East Sea Fleet maintains presence along this coast; submarine activity — including the development and testing of submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs) — has been associated with facilities in the region.

## Present-Day Marginalization

Despite its status as the second city, Hamhung receives far fewer state resources than Pyongyang. The regime's showcase investment — luxury apartment towers, ski resorts, water parks — is concentrated in and around the capital. Hamhung's infrastructure decay accelerates while the regime's narrative of prosperity is demonstrated elsewhere.

The industrial base that once defined Hamhung's importance is now largely a relic operating at reduced capacity. The Vinalon complex is intermittently functional; the fertilizer complex runs at partial output; the textile mills produce for domestic need. Chronic fuel shortages, aging Soviet-era machinery, and decades of deferred maintenance have compounded. Young people with means and connections attempt to migrate to Pyongyang; Hamhung loses human capital to the capital.

Hamhung remains strategically important to the DPRK's economy and military — its chemical production, military ordnance facilities, and ice-free port are genuine assets — but it is no longer a dynamic industrial center. It endures as a secondary city in a centralized, stagnating state: large enough to matter, peripheral enough to be neglected.
