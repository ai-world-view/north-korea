# Kaesong

**Category**: Geography

## Summary

Kaesong (개성; also romanized Gaeseong or, in older sources, Kaisung) is a city of approximately 300,000–400,000 residents in North Korea's North Hwanghae Province, situated roughly 10 kilometers north of the Korean Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) and approximately 170 kilometers south of Pyongyang. It is the northernmost major city on the Korean peninsula's western corridor, positioned astride the historic transport routes linking the peninsula's southern and northern halves. Its elevation of roughly 55 meters above sea level in a broad river valley gives it a milder climate than much of North Korea. Kaesong is one of the peninsula's oldest continuously inhabited urban centers, its settlement dating to the early Three Kingdoms period; it rose to greatest prominence as the capital of the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392), the medieval Korean state from whose name "Korea" itself derives.

In the modern era, Kaesong's border position made it a major prize and flashpoint during the [Korean War](../history/korean-war.md) of 1950–1953. After the armistice established the DMZ north of the city, Kaesong remained in North Korea and was developed as an industrial and administrative center. In the early twenty-first century the city gained international attention as the site of the Kaesong Industrial Complex (KIC), a joint North-South Korean economic zone that operated from 2004 until Seoul shut it down in 2016 amid rising tensions; proposals to revive it during the 2018 inter-Korean thaw were never realized. The KIC's history encapsulates the possibilities and limits of engagement with North Korea.

## Historical Heritage

### Goryeo Capital (918–1392)
Kaesong was established as the capital—then called Songak or Gaegyeong—by Wang Geon (Taejo of Goryeo) when he founded the Goryeo dynasty in 918. For nearly five centuries the city was the political, cultural, and commercial center of the Korean peninsula. Goryeo's sophisticated celadon pottery tradition, highly refined Buddhist temples, and the world's first metal movable type (developed at a Goryeo monastery around 1234, roughly two centuries before Gutenberg) all flourished within the city's orbit. The Goryeo capital was enclosed by a stone wall, portions of which survived into modern times and are today listed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites (inscribed 2013 as the "Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong").

The outstanding surviving heritage includes Manwoldae, the site of the Goryeo royal palace; the Koryo Museum (housed in a surviving Confucian academy building); the Sonjuk Bridge, associated with the 14th-century loyalist scholar Jeong Mong-ju who was assassinated there in 1392; and numerous Buddhist shrines. The UNESCO inscription covers twelve separate sites within and around the city.

### The Korean War
When North Korean forces launched their invasion of the South on June 25, 1950, Kaesong—then a city of approximately 100,000—was captured within hours as one of the first objectives, given its position at the border (under the 1945 partition line, the 38th parallel, Kaesong was actually within the South Korean zone, unlike Pyongyang). Over the subsequent three years, as [Korean War](../history/korean-war.md) lines shifted dramatically, Kaesong changed hands multiple times. The armistice negotiations themselves were conducted in Kaesong from July to October 1951 before moving to Panmunjom. The final armistice line, established in July 1953, placed Kaesong north of the DMZ, incorporating it into the DPRK; the city had been administered by South Korea before the war, which makes its incorporation into North Korea a singular outcome of the armistice.

### Post-War Development (1953–2000)
Under [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md)'s industrialization drive, Kaesong was developed as a manufacturing and light-industry center, leveraging its relatively dense pre-war population and surviving infrastructure. Textile mills, food-processing plants, and small-scale manufacturing were established. The city also became a significant garrison point for the [Korean People's Army](../government-politics/korean-peoples-army.md), given its proximity to the DMZ; military installations ring the city. The historic city center was largely preserved, partly for cultural reasons and partly because the city was rebuilt around industrial sites on the periphery rather than in the old core.

## The Kaesong Industrial Complex

### Origins and Structure
The Kaesong Industrial Complex (KIC; 개성공업지구) was established under agreements negotiated between the administration of South Korean President Kim Dae-jung (a key architect of the "Sunshine Policy" toward the North) and the DPRK beginning in August 2000, following the historic June 2000 inter-Korean Summit between Kim Dae-jung and [Kim Jong-il](../people/kim-jong-il.md)—the first meeting of heads of the two states since Korea's division. Construction began in June 2003; the first South Korean firm began production in December 2004. At full operation, the KIC covered approximately 66 square kilometers (with a planned eventual area of 65.7 km² plus support zones), though actual developed area peaked at around 3.3 km² for the first phase.

The complex operated under a joint management framework: the Hyundai Asan corporation and the Korea Land Corporation (KOLAND) from the South were the primary developers, providing capital and infrastructure investment; the DPRK provided land, workers, and administrative cooperation via the Kaesong Industrial District Management Committee. South Korean firms rented factory floor space and employed North Korean workers supplied through the DPRK's state labor bureau; workers were paid in hard currency (typically USD), though the currency passed through the DPRK state labor bureau which retained a substantial share, with workers receiving a fraction in won. Typical monthly wages for North Korean workers were initially around $57–65 (2004), rising toward $80–90 by 2013. At peak operation in 2015, 124 South Korean companies employed approximately 54,000 North Korean workers, primarily in textiles, electronics assembly, and light manufacturing.

### Political Disruptions and Closure
The KIC suffered several politically driven disruptions that underscored its vulnerability to inter-Korean tensions:

- **March 2009**: North Korea temporarily blocked South Korean personnel access following military exercises, suspending operations for several weeks.
- **May 2010**: Following the sinking of the South Korean naval corvette *Cheonan* on March 26, 2010 (46 sailors killed, South Korea and international investigators blamed a North Korean torpedo), South Korea's President Lee Myung-bak announced sanctions including the suspension of new investment in the KIC and a ban on South Korean travel. Existing operations continued but no new companies were admitted.
- **April–September 2013**: During a period of heightened tension following North Korea's third nuclear test (February 12, 2013) and missile activity, North Korea unilaterally withdrew its 53,000 workers and suspended KIC operations in April 2013. After negotiations, the complex reopened in September 2013 under new joint management agreements.
- **February 2016**: Following North Korea's fourth nuclear test (January 6, 2016) and a long-range rocket launch (February 7, 2016), South Korea's President Park Geun-hye announced the complete suspension of KIC operations. The last South Korean personnel left by February 11, 2016. South Korea cited national security concerns and the channeling of KIC revenue to North Korea's [nuclear weapons program](../history/nuclear-weapons-program.md).
- **2018 Thaw**: Under the Moon Jae-in administration, the April 2018 Panmunjom Declaration between Moon and Kim Jong-un included references to reviving the KIC; a September 2018 summit agreed in principle to reopen it, but UN and U.S. sanctions blocked implementation. As of mid-2026, the KIC remains closed.

### Economic Significance
At peak operation, the KIC generated roughly $120–130 million per year in hard-currency revenue for the DPRK, representing a significant share of the regime's foreign currency earnings. For South Korean companies, particularly small and medium enterprises in textiles and light manufacturing, access to North Korean workers at wages substantially below South Korean or Chinese equivalents provided a competitive advantage. The complex also demonstrated that North Korean workers could sustain quality-controlled manufacturing under foreign management—a data point often cited in assessments of the DPRK's economic potential under liberalized conditions.

The closure's economic impact on the DPRK was partially offset by expanded trade with China, but the loss of hard-currency earnings from KIC coincided with tightening international sanctions and contributed to financial pressure on the regime in 2016–2019. For the South Korean companies, the closure resulted in approximately $760 million in direct business losses; the South Korean government provided partial compensation.

## Current Status and Strategic Position

As of 2026, Kaesong remains a DPRK city fully controlled by the regime, with the KIC shuttered and fenced. The city's approximately 300,000 residents work in agriculture, light manufacturing, and military support industries. The Korean People's Army maintains substantial garrison forces in the area. The historic old city, including the UNESCO-listed monuments, is accessible only to carefully vetted visitors under DPRK-arranged itineraries, and foreign access has been essentially nonexistent since the COVID-19 border closure Kim Jong-un imposed in January 2020.

Kaesong's geographic position ensures it will remain central to any future inter-Korean negotiation. As the nearest major DPRK city to Seoul (approximately 65 kilometers), it is within potential artillery range of the South Korean capital using North Korean systems stationed in the Kaesong–Chorwon corridor. This proximity is both the source of Kaesong's strategic military value to the DPRK and the reason the KIC's existence was so politically significant: it placed thousands of South Korean citizens inside DPRK territory where they could be held as informal hostages during crises. Proposals for eventual Korean reunification consistently grapple with Kaesong's dual role as potential economic gateway and military flashpoint.

## Significance

Kaesong is significant on multiple overlapping dimensions. As the former Goryeo capital, it is a repository of Korean cultural heritage spanning a millennium, and its UNESCO listing reflects a rare area of symbolic common ground between the two Koreas (both DPRK and ROK supported the inscription). As a border city, it has been at the center of every major episode in inter-Korean military and economic relations since 1950. The Kaesong Industrial Complex, despite its ultimate failure, demonstrated that economic engagement between the two Koreas is operationally feasible and that the DPRK will accept foreign management oversight for material benefit—findings that continue to inform academic and policy debates about engagement versus isolation as a strategy toward Pyongyang. The KIC's closure in 2016 and the failure to reopen it despite the 2018 diplomatic thaw underscore how deeply the [nuclear weapons program](../history/nuclear-weapons-program.md) and sanctions architecture constrain the possibilities for inter-Korean economic cooperation.
