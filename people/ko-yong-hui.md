# Ko Yong-hui

**Category**: People

## Summary

Ko Yong-hui (고용희; 1952–2004) was the mother of North Korean leader [Kim Jong-un](kim-jong-un.md) and his siblings Kim Jong-chol and [Kim Yo-jong](kim-yo-jong.md), and the longstanding domestic partner of his predecessor, [Kim Jong-il](kim-jong-il.md). Born in 1952 to ethnic Korean parents based in Osaka, Japan, she came to North Korea as part of a repatriation program and entered Kim Jong-il's inner household in the late 1970s. Though she held no formal state title and was barely acknowledged in official media during her lifetime, she was Kim Jong-il's most important domestic partner from the 1980s until her death from breast cancer in Paris on September 13, 2004. Her significance is retrospective and derives almost entirely from the fact that her son became the DPRK's third supreme leader.

## Early Life and Background

Ko Yong-hui was born in 1952 in Osaka, Japan, to a family of Zainichi Koreans—the large community of ethnic Koreans who had been brought to Japan as laborers during the colonial period (1910–1945) and remained after Korea's liberation. Her father, Ko Gyong-taek, was reportedly a tailor. In the late 1950s and early 1960s, the Chongryun (General Association of Korean Residents in Japan, the pro-DPRK Zainichi organization) orchestrated a massive repatriation movement to North Korea, facilitated by an agreement between the DPRK and the Japanese Red Cross signed in 1959. Between 1959 and 1984, approximately 93,000 ethnic Koreans and their Japanese family members were transported to North Korea in what was presented as a return to the "socialist fatherland." Ko Yong-hui came to North Korea in this wave, likely in the early 1960s with her family.

Her life in the DPRK after repatriation is poorly documented. What is established from defector sources and intelligence assessments is that she trained as a dancer and became a performer in the Mansudae Art Troupe—the DPRK's premier artistic ensemble, responsible for mass performances and propaganda spectacles. This position gave her access to elite circles. Her meeting with Kim Jong-il, whose interest in cinema and the arts was well-documented, is believed to have occurred through this cultural setting in the mid-to-late 1970s.

## Relationship with Kim Jong-il

Ko Yong-hui's relationship with Kim Jong-il placed her third in a succession of women who served as his domestic partners, following his first wife Kim Yong-suk (who bore daughter Kim Sol-song), and the actress Song Hye-rim (who bore Kim Jong-il's eldest son, Kim Jong-nam). Unlike these earlier relationships, Ko Yong-hui's household became the acknowledged domestic center of Kim Jong-il's private life in the 1980s and 1990s, and the children she bore him—Kim Jong-chol (born 1981), Kim Jong-un (born approximately 1983–1984), and Kim Yo-jong (born 1987)—were eventually positioned as the legitimate heirs.

Kim Jong-il's domestic arrangements were kept entirely secret during [Kim Il-sung](kim-il-sung.md)'s lifetime; the elder Kim disapproved of his son's personal conduct. Ko Yong-hui did not appear in official media and her name was not publicly associated with Kim Jong-il. Within the inner court, she was addressed as "respected mother" (존경하는 어머니) by staff, a formulation later used in loyalty campaigns directed at Kim Jong-un's generation, and she wielded the practical authority of the household matriarch.

## Role in the Succession Process

Ko Yong-hui's most consequential role came in the 2000s, when [Kim Jong-il](kim-jong-il.md)'s health and the question of succession grew increasingly urgent. Kim Jong-il suffered a reported stroke in August 2008 (which led to visible physical decline by that autumn), but even before that crisis, the competition among possible heirs—Kim Jong-nam (son of Sung Hye-rim), Kim Jong-chol, and Kim Jong-un—was being resolved in the inner court. Intelligence assessments and defector accounts indicate that Ko Yong-hui was a consistent advocate for her own sons, particularly Kim Jong-un, and worked to marginalize Kim Jong-nam's prospects. Kim Jong-nam had already embarrassed the regime by attempting to travel to Japan on a forged Dominican Republic passport in May 2001 (reportedly to visit Tokyo Disneyland), an episode that damaged his standing with Kim Jong-il.

Ko Yong-hui did not live to see her son designated as heir: she died of breast cancer on September 13, 2004, in a Paris hospital where she had been receiving treatment. Kim Jong-un's formal designation came only in September 2010, when he was appointed vice-chairman of the Korean Workers' Party's Central Military Commission at the party conference—a signal to the elite that he was the chosen successor. Kim Jong-il died in December 2011.

## Death and Posthumous Status

Ko Yong-hui died in Paris at the age of approximately 52, from breast cancer for which she had been receiving treatment in France since 2003. Her death was not publicly announced in North Korea. Kim Jong-il reportedly entered a period of deep mourning; defector accounts describe his grief as profound.

After Kim Jong-un consolidated power following Kim Jong-il's death in December 2011, the regime undertook a limited posthumous elevation of Ko Yong-hui. State media began referring to her obliquely as "the mother of Pyongyang" and "the great mother of the army." In 2012 a documentary apparently titled *Mother of Great Songun Korea* was broadcast internally, presenting her as a devoted revolutionary figure. However, she was never given the full posthumous titles and official biography accorded to [Kim Il-sung](kim-il-sung.md) or Kim Jong-il; her Japanese birth and Zainichi background were politically sensitive, given North Korea's official nationalism and the regime's complex relationship with the Zainichi community. The regime never publicly confirmed her name or biography in mainstream media consumed outside the DPRK.

## The Mythology of Kim Jong-un's Birth

One specific consequence of Ko Yong-hui's biography is the contradiction it creates with the official mythology surrounding [Kim Jong-un](kim-jong-un.md)'s birth. Official DPRK sources assert that Kim Jong-un was born in 1982 at a secret revolutionary camp on [Mount Paektu](../geography/mount-paektu.md), the volcanic mountain on the Chinese border held sacred in the regime's ideology as the birthplace of Kim Jong-il and the origin point of Korean liberation mythology. Intelligence assessments and defector accounts place Ko Yong-hui and her family in Pyongyang and abroad (including in Switzerland) during the years of Kim Jong-un's birth, which is believed to be 1983 or 1984. The official birthdate of 1982 and the Mount Paektu narrative are understood by analysts to be retroactive fabrications intended to invest Kim Jong-un with the revolutionary legitimacy that Kim Jong-il had claimed for himself. Ko Yong-hui's actual biography—Japanese-born, Zainichi community background, Paris hospital death—sits in sharp contrast to the heroic mythology the regime has constructed around her son.

## Significance

Ko Yong-hui is significant primarily as the biological progenitor of the Kim dynasty's third generation and as an indirect witness to the gap between the DPRK's official mythology and documented reality. Her biography challenges the Mount Paektu birth narrative, illuminates the domestic succession politics of the Kim court, and illustrates how women have functioned in North Korea's elite: wielding real influence through kinship and household management while remaining officially invisible. Her death in Paris rather than in North Korea—a symbol of the elite's access to foreign medical care unavailable to the general population—contrasts sharply with the regime's nationalist propaganda about the superiority of the North Korean system.

Her son [Kim Jong-un](kim-jong-un.md)'s consolidation of power after 2011, including the execution of his uncle Jang Song-thaek in December 2013 and the assassination of half-brother Kim Jong-nam in Kuala Lumpur in February 2017, can be partly read as the culmination of the succession competition in which Ko Yong-hui's advocacy played a foundational role. Her daughter [Kim Yo-jong](kim-yo-jong.md) has emerged as among the most powerful figures in the current regime, serving as Kim Jong-un's closest confidante and a key [Workers' Party of Korea](../government-politics/workers-party-of-korea.md) official—a second-generation consequence of Ko Yong-hui's place in the Kim household.
