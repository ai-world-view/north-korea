# Kim Jong-il

**Category**: People
**Birth**: February 16, 1942 (Vyatskoye, USSR, near Khabarovsk; officially claimed as Paektu Mountain, Korea)
**Death**: December 17, 2011 (Pyongyang, DPRK)
**Tenure**: Chairman of the National Defense Commission 1993–2011; General Secretary of the WPK 1997–2011; Supreme Leader 1994–2011

## Overview

Kim Jong-il (김정일) was the second leader of North Korea, ruling for 17 years from his father [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md)'s death in 1994 until his own death in 2011. He is credited with the formalization of the **Songun** (military-first) policy that elevated the Korean People's Army to the supreme institution of the state and introduced cult-of-personality glorification of both himself and his father. Under his rule, North Korea conducted its first nuclear weapons test (2006) and developed a rudimentary nuclear arsenal. Simultaneously, his tenure encompassed the catastrophic famine of the 1990s (1994–1998), which killed an estimated 600,000–1 million people.

Kim Jong-il is remembered internationally as an eccentric figure, partly due to unusual biographical details (his claimed location of birth, his reported devotion to cinema, rumors of health eccentricities). However, his rule was ruthless and his accumulation of absolute power was methodical, mirroring his father's consolidation but executed more efficiently. His succession to his young son Kim Jong-un in 2011 established the unprecedented transition of power to a third generation of the Kim dynasty.

## Early Life and Soviet Background

Kim Jong-il was born on **February 16, 1942**, in **Vyatskoye** (Вятское), a village near Khabarovsk in the Soviet Far East, where his father's unit was based. His mother was **Kim Jong-suk** (1917–1949), [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md)'s first wife and a celebrated revolutionary heroine in North Korean mythology (she appears in DPRK propaganda as the "Sacred Mother of Korea"). His father was commanding the Korean-Chinese unit of the Soviet 88th Special Reconnaissance Brigade.

The official North Korean account claims Kim Jong-il was born on **Paektu Mountain** (백두산 — the sacred peak on the Korean-Chinese border venerated in Korean mythology) on the same date, surrounded by a double rainbow and a bright star, and that soldiers on the mountain announced his birth with celebratory gunfire. These embellishments consciously mirror Korean messianic mythology and were designed to anchor Kim Jong-il's legitimacy in sacred national geography. Paektu Mountain features extensively in DPRK propaganda: Kim Jong-un has made multiple symbolic visits to the site to reinforce dynastic legitimacy.

Kim Jong-il spent his early childhood in the Soviet Union until August 1945, when Soviet forces entered northern Korea following Japan's surrender. He moved to Korea with his family at age 3. He had a younger brother, **Kim Man-il** (known by the Russian nickname "Shura," born 1944), who reportedly drowned in a pool in 1947 while [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md) was absent. Whether this accident was as described or resulted from other circumstances was never independently confirmed.

His mother, Kim Jong-suk, died on **September 22, 1949**, in childbirth (bearing a stillborn daughter), when Kim Jong-il was 7. Her death left an imprint on his psychological development and was later mythologized. [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md) subsequently married **Kim Song-ae** (1926–2014) around 1952–1953, producing a half-brother named **Kim Pyong-il** (born 1954) and other children — creating a potential succession rivalry that Kim Jong-il would later manage strategically.

## Grooming for Power (1956–1980)

Kim Il-sung had only one son from his first marriage — Kim Jong-il — ensuring clear succession. However, Kim Il-sung also had children from his second marriage to Kim Song-ae (married circa 1953), including Kim Pyong-il (born 1954). The potential rivalry between Kim Jong-il and his half-brother was managed through diplomatic exile: Kim Pyong-il served as ambassador to various Eastern European countries for decades, effectively removed from the succession contest.

**Education**: Kim Jong-il was educated in elite schools in Pyongyang and the Soviet Union. He attended the Soviet-affiliated school in Pyongyang and spoke Russian as well as Korean. Reports suggest he was an average student, showing interest in film and the arts more than in academic subjects.

**Party Cadre** (1960s): In the early 1960s, as the party youth section expanded, Kim Jong-il was placed in administrative positions within the Workers' Party of Korea. He was appointed to the Central Committee's Propaganda and Agitation Department, which controlled media, ideology, and the personality cult. His responsibility for propaganda and cultural affairs gave him influence over the state's ideological apparatus from his 20s onward.

**Personality Cult Management** (1960s–1970s): From the mid-1960s, Kim Jong-il directed the expansion of his father's personality cult and began cultivating his own image in parallel. He emphasized his father's revolutionary legacy, introduced more elaborate titles and hagiographies, and began the process of making himself indispensable to state functioning.

**Politburo and Succession** (1974–1980): In 1974, at age 32, Kim Jong-il was elevated to the Politburo of the Workers' Party — the innermost decision-making circle. He held the unofficial title of "Dear Leader" (widely used within North Korea, though never officially adopted as a constitutional title). At the **6th Party Congress in October 1980**, he was publicly designated as [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md)'s successor, formally and in front of international observers.

The next 14 years (1980–1994) saw Kim Jong-il gradually consolidate power while his aging father remained the symbolic head of state. He took on increasing responsibility for economic policy, military coordination, and day-to-day governance.

## Personality Cult and Personal Mythology

Kim Jong-il cultivated an image distinct from his father's. While [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md) was portrayed as a warrior-revolutionary and founding father, Kim Jong-il was presented as a brilliant cultural theorist, military strategist, and organizational genius. Key elements of his cult included:

**The *Ten Principles* (1974)** — A document that formalized the state ideology of absolute devotion to the Kim family. It asserts that party members and citizens must study the Kims' teachings, maintain their loyalty, follow their instructions without question, and propagate the ideology to all. Violation of these principles carries severe penalties, and the document remains binding state law.

**Filial Piety**: Kim Jong-il was framed as the perfect son who inherited his father's revolutionary legacy and carried it forward. This resonated with Confucian values still embedded in Korean culture, despite the state's nominally anti-Confucian stance.

**Cultural and Artistic Genius**: Kim Jong-il cultivated a genuine passion for cinema, reportedly accumulating a private collection of over 20,000 films on VHS and laser disc, including a significant number of Western Hollywood productions. He published a treatise *On the Art of the Cinema* (1973, officially), in which he articulated theories of socialist realism in film. He took a direct role in North Korean film production, directing or supervising several productions and insisting on high production values by DPRK standards.

His obsession with cinema drove one of the most audacious acts of personal cultural intervention in the Kim era: in 1978, he ordered the **abduction of South Korean filmmaker Shin Sang-ok and actress Choe Eun-hee**, who were separately kidnapped from Hong Kong by North Korean operatives and brought to Pyongyang. Kim Jong-il tasked Shin with revitalizing the North Korean film industry. Shin produced seven films under North Korean supervision between 1978 and 1986 (including the monster film *Pulgasari*, 1985, a work of socialist allegory using a Godzilla-style creature). In 1986, Shin and Choe escaped during a visit to Vienna, eventually reaching the US and revealing the full story of their captivity.

**Military Acumen**: As he took on greater coordination with the Korean People's Army, he was credited with strategic brilliance and military innovation, though he had no actual military service or battlefield experience.

**Physical Mythology**: Rumors, partly spread by the regime and partly by international observers, created an aura around his physical person: claims that he did not defecate, that he could control the weather, that he was an extraordinarily talented golfer (supposedly shooting an 11 for 18 holes on his first try), and that he had no bodily hair. These claims were official propaganda mixed with Orientalist fascination in the West.

## Supreme Leadership (1994–2011)

**Succession Crisis and Consolidation** (1994): [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md) died on July 8, 1994. Kim Jong-il did not assume the title of President or Premier, leaving those positions symbolically vacant (the 1998 constitution eventually declared Kim Il-sung "Eternal President," institutionalizing the vacancy). He instead held the title of General Secretary of the WPK (from October 1997) and, from April 1993, Chairman of the National Defense Commission — a post the revised 1998 constitution declared the "highest organ of state power." This unusual arrangement reflected a deliberate shift in state organizational structure: the National Defense Commission, formerly a secondary body, became supreme, elevating military affairs to the center of governance.

There was a prescribed three-year mourning period before Kim Jong-il assumed formal party leadership titles (he held day-to-day power from 1994 but did not take the formal WPK General Secretaryship until October 1997). This period coincided exactly with the worst years of the famine (1994–1997), creating a political-humanitarian disaster that Kim Jong-il inherited and managed through Songun priorities.

The transition was smooth at the elite level, in part because Kim Jong-il had been effectively governing for years, but marked by economic crisis: the end of Soviet subsidies, the collapse of the planned economy, and the onset of the catastrophic [famine](../economy/economic-system-and-famine.md).

**Military-First (Songun) Policy** (mid-1990s): In response to economic collapse and the withdrawal of external support, Kim Jong-il elevated the Korean People's Army to a position of supreme importance in the state. The **Songun policy** asserted that military security was the prerequisite for all state functions. Military spending was prioritized over civilian consumption; military officers gained prominence in the party and state apparatus; and the military's institutional interests were centered in decision-making.

The Songun policy reflected strategic calculation (given the perception of threat from a much wealthier South and its US ally) but it also had catastrophic humanitarian consequences: during the famine years (1994–1998), military rations were maintained while the civilian population starved. The regime's refusal to reduce military spending during a humanitarian crisis demonstrated the priority given to security over survival.

**Famine and International Engagement** (1994–2000): The first six years of Kim Jong-il's rule coincided with the worst phases of the famine. He gradually accepted international humanitarian assistance, particularly from the UN World Food Programme and from South Korea. He also opened limited economic zones (Rajin-Sonbong, later Rason) as experiments with market mechanisms, though these had limited economic impact.

**Nuclear Weapons Program** (1993–2006): North Korea had begun a nuclear weapons research program in the 1980s under Soviet and Chinese technical guidance. Under Kim Jong-il, the program accelerated. In October 2002, the US disclosed that North Korea had been conducting a secret highly enriched uranium program, in violation of the 1994 Agreed Framework (which had frozen plutonium production). This triggered international crisis.

On **October 9, 2006**, North Korea conducted its first nuclear weapons test, detonating a device with an estimated yield of 4–20 kilotons in an underground chamber beneath Mount Mantap in the northeast. This was followed by additional tests in 2009, 2013 (twice), 2016 (twice), and 2017. The tests demonstrated that North Korea possessed functioning nuclear weapons and was developing delivery systems (missiles).

The nuclearization of the peninsula under Kim Jong-il was presented domestically as vindication of the Songun policy and proof that North Korea's military-first strategy had achieved deterrence. Internationally, it elevated North Korea's strategic importance and leverage in negotiations.

**Six-Party Talks** (2003–2008): In response to the nuclear crisis, China convened multilateral negotiations including the US, Russia, Japan, South Korea, and North Korea. The talks were intermittently productive: in 2005, North Korea agreed to abandon its nuclear program in exchange for energy aid and diplomatic normalization. However, disputes over verification and the pace of sanctions relief led to the breakdown of the talks in 2008.

**State-Sponsored Terrorism (1983–1987)**: During the transition years under Kim Il-sung but while Kim Jong-il exercised substantial operational influence, North Korea conducted two significant terrorist acts attributed to his direction:

- **October 9, 1983 — Rangoon Bombing**: North Korean operatives planted a bomb at the Martyr's Mausoleum in Rangoon, Burma, targeting South Korean President Chun Doo-hwan during an official visit. Chun survived (he arrived late), but 21 people were killed, including four South Korean cabinet ministers. Burma expelled the North Korean ambassador and broke diplomatic relations.
- **November 29, 1987 — Korean Air Flight 858**: North Korean agents planted a bomb in the overhead compartment of KAL Flight 858, which exploded over the Andaman Sea between Abu Dhabi and Bangkok, killing all 115 people aboard. North Korean agent **Kim Hyun-hui** was arrested in Bahrain and eventually provided a detailed confession to South Korean intelligence. The bombing was intended to deter attendance at the 1988 Seoul Olympics.

**Inter-Korean Relations**: Kim Jong-il pursued a nuanced policy toward South Korea that mixed confrontation with selective engagement. He authorized two major attacks on South Korean military targets (the **Cheonan warship sinking** on March 26, 2010, killing 46 sailors, attributed to a North Korean torpedo, and the **Yeonpyeong Island bombardment** on November 23, 2010, killing 4 South Koreans), killing nearly 50 South Korean military personnel and civilians in that period. Simultaneously, he had permitted the Kaesong Industrial Complex (opened 2004), where South Korean companies contracted North Korean labor, and engaged in the historic **June 15, 2000 inter-Korean summit** with South Korean President Kim Dae-jung in [Pyongyang](../geography/pyongyang.md) — the first meeting between leaders of the two Koreas since the 1953 armistice. Family reunions, suspended periodically, brought separated families together under state-controlled conditions.

**Military Modernization**: Under Kim Jong-il, the Korean People's Army underwent significant modernization in some sectors (particularly the Strategic Rocket Force), though much of the military remained equipped with aging Soviet-era hardware. The development of ballistic missiles (Rodong, Taepodong series) and the nuclear weapons program were Kim Jong-il's signature military achievements.

## Personal Life and Family

Kim Jong-il's personal life was deliberately obscured from public view, but substantial information emerged through defector accounts and diplomatic reporting after his death.

He had at least four relationships producing heirs:

1. **Kim Young-suk** (김영숙, born 1947) — his official wife, a marriage arranged by Kim Il-sung around 1974. They reportedly had little personal warmth and lived separately for much of their marriage. She did not produce a recognized heir who entered the succession contest.

2. **Song Hye-rim** (성혜림, 1937–2002) — an actress Kim Jong-il began an affair with in the late 1960s. The relationship was concealed from Kim Il-sung for years. She bore Kim Jong-il's eldest son, **Kim Jong-nam** (김정남, 1971–2017). Kim Jong-nam was considered a potential heir but fell out of favor after being caught attempting to enter Japan in 2001 on a false passport (reportedly to visit Tokyo Disneyland). He lived in Macau as a semi-exile with Chinese protection; he was assassinated on February 13, 2017, at Kuala Lumpur International Airport by two women who smeared his face with VX nerve agent — an act widely attributed to North Korean intelligence.

3. **Ko Yong-hui** (고용희, 1952–2004) — a dancer of Zainichi Korean (ethnic Korean resident in Japan) background, and Kim Jong-il's most favored companion in his later years. She bore two sons: **Kim Jong-chul** (김정철, born 1981) and **Kim Jong-un** (김정은, born approximately January 8, 1983 or 1984). She died of breast cancer in Paris in 2004.

4. **Kim Ok** (김옥) — a personal secretary who became Kim Jong-il's companion in his final years and was reportedly present at his death in December 2011.

## Health and Power Consolidation

By the 2000s, reports emerged that Kim Jong-il had suffered health problems, including a suspected **stroke in August 2008**, after which he was absent from public view for two months. He returned to public activities by late 2008 but showed visible signs of impairment (dragging his left foot, facial drooping). He reduced public appearances and concentrated power in a small inner circle of family members, military officials, and trusted party cadres. He groomed his youngest son, **Kim Jong-un** (born c. 1983–1984), for succession.

In September 2010, at the 3rd Conference of the Workers' Party (a rare gathering of party elites), Kim Jong-il formally presented Kim Jong-un as his successor, elevating him to four-star general and key party posts. This move, which seemed to sideline other potential heirs (including two other sons), ensured the continuation of Kim family rule and the succession to a third generation.

## Death and Legacy

Kim Jong-il died on **December 17, 2011**, at age 69. Official reports attributed his death to a heart attack while traveling by train, though international observers speculated about various health conditions. His death was announced three days later, triggering another period of managed mourning and public displays of grief.

His body was embalmed and placed in the Kumsusan Palace of the Sun alongside his father's, making it the world's largest mausoleum to two deceased leaders. The 1998 constitution was amended to declare Kim Il-sung the "Eternal President," but Kim Jong-il did not receive an equivalent title; instead, he is honored as a "Eternal General Secretary" of the party and his leadership period is treated as the foundational phase of Songun ideology.

**Domestic Legacy** — Within North Korea, Kim Jong-il is credited with consolidating the state's military power, achieving nuclear deterrence, and stabilizing the regime through the crisis of the 1990s. His emphasis on Songun and the military remains official policy under his son.

**International Legacy** — Internationally, Kim Jong-il is remembered as an eccentric despot who pursued nuclear weapons while his population starved, who engaged in occasional acts of international terrorism and provocations, and who created a totalitarian state machinery of extraordinary surveillance and control. His eccentricities — the reported golfing feats, the film collection, the fashion sense (famously including platform shoes to add to his height) — became subjects of international curiosity and mockery, though the underlying reality of his rule was deeply tragic.

**Succession** — Most significantly, Kim Jong-il established the precedent that the Kim family dictatorship could survive the transition to a third generation of leadership, ensuring the dynasty's continuity beyond his own lifetime.
