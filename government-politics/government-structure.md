# Government Structure

**Category**: Government & Politics
**Key figures**: [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md) (founder), Kim Jong-il, Kim Jong-un

## Overview

North Korea — formally the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK) — operates as a totalitarian one-party state under the hereditary leadership of the Kim dynasty. All political authority derives from the Workers' Party of Korea (WPK), which controls the state apparatus, the military, and every organized institution in society. In practice, supreme power is concentrated in the person of the Supreme Leader, whose decisions carry the force of law and are executed through overlapping party, state, and military structures.

The system has its roots in the Soviet-backed provisional administration established after 1945. It was formalized by the constitution of September 8, 1948 — the day before the DPRK was officially proclaimed — and has been amended substantially in 1972, 1992, 1998, 2009, 2012, 2016, and 2019, each revision adjusting the formal distribution of power to reflect the actual authority of the incumbent leader.

## Constitutional Framework

North Korea's constitution describes the DPRK as "a socialist state of the working people." The document combines Marxist-Leninist language with distinctly Korean nationalist framing tied to the Kim family. Several provisions are largely nominal:

- The constitution nominally guarantees freedoms of speech, press, assembly, and religion, but these rights are circumscribed by the requirement that they not "harm socialist interests."
- The Supreme People's Assembly is described as the highest state power organ, but it meets only a few days per year and has never defeated a government proposal.
- Property rights are limited; all significant means of production, land, and natural resources are owned by the state or cooperatives.

Key constitutional revisions include the **1972 revision**, which created the office of President of the Republic (held by Kim Il-sung until his death), and the **1998 revision**, which declared Kim Il-sung the "Eternal President," effectively removing the living presidency and reorganizing state authority under the National Defense Commission (NDC). The **2016 revision** replaced the NDC with the **State Affairs Commission (SAC)**, which Kim Jong-un chairs.

## The Supreme Leader

The Supreme Leader holds authority exceeding any single formal title. Kim Jong-un, as of 2021, simultaneously holds the following positions:

- **General Secretary of the Workers' Party of Korea** — the highest party post, reinstated at the 8th Party Congress in January 2021 (the title had lapsed since Kim Il-sung's era).
- **Chairman of the State Affairs Commission** — the constitutional head-of-state equivalent, presiding over the highest executive body.
- **Supreme Commander of the Korean People's Army** — direct command over all armed forces.

This tripartite concentration mirrors [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md)'s arrangement from the 1970s and consolidates all levers of power — party, state, and military — in one person. Decisions from the Supreme Leader are transmitted through party organs and do not require legislative approval.

## Workers' Party of Korea

The Workers' Party of Korea (WPK) is the sole ruling party, founded on October 10, 1945, shortly after Soviet forces entered northern Korea. It claims a membership of approximately 3–4 million (in a population of roughly 26 million). All significant state officials are WPK members, and membership is essential for social advancement.

**Internal structure:**

| Organ | Role |
|---|---|
| Party Congress | Nominal supreme authority; meets rarely (7th Congress 2016, 8th Congress Jan 2021) |
| Central Committee | Directs party affairs between Congresses; has ~300 full and candidate members |
| Political Bureau (Politburo) | Core decision-making body; ~30–40 members |
| Politburo Presidium / Standing Committee | Most senior leaders; 5–7 members |
| Secretariat | Administers day-to-day party operations |
| Control Commission | Enforces party discipline |

The Politburo's Presidium (also called the Standing Committee) functions as the real cabinet of North Korea. It formulates major policies in economics, military affairs, foreign relations, and ideology.

**Organizational departments** of the Central Committee handle specific functions, including:

- **Organization and Guidance Department (OGD)** — the most powerful internal party bureau; controls personnel appointments at all levels, monitors officials, and runs the political officer network throughout the military and state agencies.
- **Propaganda and Agitation Department** — manages mass media, ideology education, and the personality cult of the Kim family.
- **United Front Department** — oversees relations with South Korea and overseas Korean communities, including Chongryon (the General Association of Korean Residents in Japan).
- **Military Affairs Department** — coordinates between party leadership and the Korean People's Army.

## Supreme People's Assembly

The Supreme People's Assembly (SPA) is nominally the highest legislative organ. As of the 2019 elections it has **687 deputies**, all nominated by the Democratic Front for the Reunification of the Fatherland (a WPK-led coalition). Elections are held every five years with a single candidate per constituency; reported turnout is consistently near 100 percent.

The SPA meets typically twice per year for sessions lasting one to two days. It formally ratifies the state budget, major legislation, and constitutional amendments. It elects (in name) the President of the Presidium of the SPA — a largely ceremonial head-of-state role for external diplomacy — and approves the Cabinet.

In practice the SPA functions as a rubber-stamp body, approving without debate all proposals submitted by the party leadership. No record of a dissenting vote exists.

## Cabinet (내각, Naegak)

The Cabinet, headed by the Premier, manages day-to-day state administration including economic planning, industrial ministries, agriculture, and public services. It operates under the guidance of the SAC and ultimately under the Supreme Leader's directives. The Cabinet includes approximately 30 ministries and commissions. The Premier (Prime Minister) is a senior WPK official appointed by the SPA.

Unlike in many states, the Cabinet holds less real power than the party apparatus, as the WPK's departments shadow and supervise each ministry's activities.

## Military: Korean People's Army

The Korean People's Army (KPA) is one of the world's largest standing militaries with approximately **1.28 million active-duty personnel** (and roughly 600,000 reserves), representing about 5 percent of the population. The KPA consists of the Ground Force, Navy, Air and Anti-Air Force, Strategic Rocket Force, and Special Operations Force.

The KPA answers directly to the Supreme Commander (the Supreme Leader). Political officers appointed by the WPK's Organization and Guidance Department are embedded at every level of the military hierarchy, ensuring party loyalty takes precedence over operational chains of command.

Kim Jong-il's **Songun** (선군, "military-first") policy, formally articulated in the mid-1990s, elevated the KPA's institutional role in governance. Under Songun, resources were prioritized for the military even during the famine of the mid-1990s. Kim Jong-un has maintained the emphasis on military capability while also reasserting party supremacy over the military (reversing a degree of military autonomy that accumulated under Songun).

The **Strategic Rocket Force** (formerly the Strategic Rocket Forces Command), which manages nuclear-capable ballistic missiles, is particularly significant in the context of North Korea's deterrence strategy. The country conducted its first nuclear test on October 9, 2006, and has since conducted five additional tests (2009, 2013, 2016 ×2, 2017).

## Security Apparatus

Internal security is maintained by overlapping agencies designed to prevent dissent and ensure loyalty:

**State Security Department (국가보위성, Gukgabowibu / Bowibu):** The political secret police, analogous to the Soviet KGB. It monitors citizens, officials, and foreigners for disloyalty, ideological deviation, and contact with the outside world. It operates the network of political prison camps (kwanliso), including notorious facilities at Yodok (Camp 15), Hwasong (Camp 16), and Kaechon (Camp 14). An estimated 80,000–120,000 prisoners are held in these camps.

**Ministry for Social Security (사회안전성):** The regular police force, handling crime and public order. Also maintains a surveillance network and operates the less severe "re-education" camps (kyohwaso) for ordinary criminal offenses.

**Reconnaissance General Bureau:** Oversees foreign intelligence, covert operations, and cyberwarfare units (including the Lazarus Group, linked to major cyberattacks and cryptocurrency thefts).

The overlapping mandates of these agencies — and their direct reporting to the Supreme Leader rather than to one another — create competitive surveillance that discourages any single institution from accumulating excessive independent power.

## Juche Ideology

Juche (주체, meaning "self-reliance" or "subject") is the official state ideology, developed by [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md) and formally enshrined in the 1972 constitution. Its core principles hold that the human being is the master of all things, that Korea must be independent in ideology (chaju), self-sufficient in the economy (charip), and self-reliant in national defense (chawi).

In practice, Juche provides the ideological justification for:

- Isolation from foreign economic integration.
- Resistance to international pressure on human rights or nuclear weapons.
- The supreme leader's role as the embodiment of the people's will.
- The hereditary succession, framed as natural continuation of revolutionary leadership.

The philosopher and party theorist Hwang Jang-yop (who defected to South Korea in 1997) is credited with systematizing Juche into a formal philosophical doctrine in the 1970s, though official historiography attributes the ideology entirely to Kim Il-sung.

A secondary ideology, **Kimilsungism-Kimjongilism**, was added to the constitution in 2012 to explicitly incorporate Kim Jong-il's contributions alongside those of his father.

## Regional Administration

North Korea is divided into:

- **9 provinces** (도, do): North Hamgyong, South Hamgyong, Ryanggang, North Pyongan, South Pyongan, North Hwanghae, South Hwanghae, Kangwon, Chagang.
- **2 special cities** directly under central government: Pyongyang (the capital, population approximately 3 million) and Rason (Rajin-Sonbong, a special economic zone).
- **3 special administrative regions**: Kaesong Industrial Zone (operations suspended since 2016), Kumgangsan Tourist Region, and Sinuiju Special Administrative Region.

Each province is administered by a people's committee and a parallel WPK provincial committee. The provincial WPK secretary effectively outranks the administrative governor. All regional officials are appointed by the central party apparatus and must demonstrate consistent loyalty to maintain their positions.

Pyongyang enjoys privileged status: its residents receive preferential access to food, housing, consumer goods, and education. Assignment to Pyongyang residence is a reward for loyalty and performance; removal from the capital is a form of punishment.

## Succession and Dynastic Continuity

The Kim family's hereditary succession has no parallel among socialist states. Power has passed twice:

1. **Kim Il-sung → Kim Jong-il**: Kim Jong-il was publicly designated heir at the 6th Party Congress in October 1980, having been groomed since 1974. He assumed full control after Kim Il-sung's death on July 8, 1994.
2. **Kim Jong-il → Kim Jong-un**: Kim Jong-un (born January 8, 1983 or 1984) was appointed as a four-star general and to key party positions in September 2010. He assumed supreme leadership following Kim Jong-il's death on December 17, 2011.

Each succession has been managed through the party and military apparatus as a quasi-constitutional transfer, and each successor has used a combination of loyalty-building, purges of potential rivals, and ideological legitimation to consolidate power. Kim Jong-un's purge and execution of his uncle Jang Song-thaek in December 2013 — a previously powerful party and state official — was the most dramatic consolidation of his rule.
