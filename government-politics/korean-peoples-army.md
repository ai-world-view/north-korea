# Korean People's Army

The **Korean People's Army** (조선인민군, KPA) is the military wing of the DPRK and the institutional pillar of the regime's totalitarian control. Formally founded on February 8, 1948 — a date celebrated annually as KPA founding day — it serves simultaneously as the country's armed force, a vast economic conglomerate, and a political institution through which the Kim dynasty maintains absolute power over society. With approximately 1.2 million active-duty personnel, the KPA is the world's fourth-largest standing military by personnel, fielded by a country with a GDP estimated at US$17–28 billion.

## Origins and Early History

The KPA traces its ideological lineage to the **anti-Japanese guerrilla forces** led by Kim Il-sung in Manchuria during the 1930s and 1940s. In the official DPRK narrative, these partisan units — the cornerstone myth of Kim Il-sung's revolutionary legitimacy — are the founding kernel of the military. In practice, the institutional KPA emerged from Soviet occupation structures: after Soviet forces entered northern Korea in August 1945, the Korean Peoples' Guard Corps (조선보안대) was established in October 1945 under Soviet military oversight, drawing on Koreans who had served in Soviet Red Army units, Chinese Communist forces, and the remnants of anti-Japanese guerrilla bands.

The **Korean People's Army** was formally constituted on February 8, 1948, six months before the founding of the DPRK itself. The date's selection — preceding the September 9, 1948 founding of the state — reflects the regime's doctrine that the military preceded and undergirds the political order.

### The Korean War (1950–1953)

The KPA launched the Korean War with a dawn invasion of South Korea on **June 25, 1950** at 4:00 AM, striking south across the 38th parallel with approximately 135,000 troops organized in 7 divisions, supported by 150 Soviet-supplied T-34 tanks. The DPRK's official designation — "The Fatherland Liberation War" — frames this as a defensive response to South Korean provocation, a narrative contradicted by documentary evidence including captured Soviet military planning documents.

KPA forces rapidly overran Seoul within three days and pushed UN and South Korean forces into the **Pusan Perimeter** by August 1950. The subsequent **UN counteroffensive** (September 1950), led by General Douglas MacArthur's Inchon landing, collapsed KPA overextension; most of the original invasion force was destroyed or dispersed. By October 1950, UN forces had crossed the 38th parallel and approached the Chinese border.

Chinese intervention in late October 1950 — the People's Liberation Army (PLA) deploying approximately 300,000 troops — altered the conflict decisively. The KPA, having suffered catastrophic losses in the first months, was reconstituted from Chinese-supplied replacements and newly mobilized conscripts. The subsequent three years of grinding positional warfare along the 38th parallel culminated in the **Armistice Agreement of July 27, 1953** — an armistice, not a peace treaty, leaving a technical state of war that the DPRK has since used to justify permanent military mobilization.

KPA losses in the war were immense: estimates range from 215,000 to 520,000 KPA soldiers killed, with total Korean civilian deaths estimated at 2–3 million. The war's trauma permanently shaped the DPRK's military doctrine, framing the US–South Korea alliance as an existential threat requiring absolute military preparedness.

## Structure and Command

The KPA operates under a layered command structure:

**Supreme Commander**: The Supreme Leader (Kim Jong-un, holding the title since December 2011). Command authority is constitutionally absolute and personalized; Kim signs off on nuclear tests, missile launches, and strategic deployments personally.

**Central Military Commission of the Workers' Party of Korea** (WPK CMC): The highest policy body for military affairs, chaired by Kim Jong-un. WPK primacy over the military — deliberately emphasized by Kim Jong-un since 2012 — means the party formally controls the army rather than the reverse (reversing the de facto Songun-era drift under Kim Jong-il).

**State Affairs Commission** (formerly the National Defense Commission): The executive coordinating body for national security affairs.

**General Staff Department**: The operational arm responsible for day-to-day command of forces.

### Branches

| Branch | Approximate Personnel | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ground Forces | ~950,000 | Largest component; 20 corps-level commands |
| Air and Anti-Air Force | ~110,000 | Primarily Soviet-era aircraft; limited operational capability |
| Navy | ~60,000 | Primarily coastal defense; active submarine fleet |
| Strategic Forces Command | ~10,000+ | Established 2012; manages nuclear and ballistic missile systems |
| Special Operations Forces | ~200,000 | Unusually large; light infantry, infiltration, and unconventional warfare |

Special Operations Forces (SOF) are a particular priority: North Korea maintains one of the largest special operations establishments in the world, trained for infiltration of South Korea via tunnels, submarines, and light aircraft. During a conflict, SOF units would be tasked with decapitation strikes, sabotage of South Korean infrastructure, and disruption of US reinforcement operations.

## Conscription and Personnel

Conscription is universal and unavoidable for males, and has expanded to encompass females. Key parameters:

- **Male service obligation**: typically 8–10 years; effectively open-ended during periods when the military holds back personnel
- **Female service**: made formally mandatory in 2015 for women aged 17–20 under Kim Jong-un, with service terms of 5–7 years; previously "voluntary" in practice
- **Age of induction**: 17 years old at recruitment; secondary school military training begins earlier through the Red Guards
- **Total mobilizable forces**: approximately 7–8 million when reserves are included

Promotion follows political loyalty as much as military competence; party affiliation, **songbun** class, and genealogical standing determine advancement to officer grades. The officer corps — especially at senior levels — is drawn overwhelmingly from "core" songbun families with documented revolutionary credentials.

Living conditions for ordinary conscripts are harsh. Malnutrition among soldiers reflects national food scarcity: unit rations have reportedly been cut multiple times during economic crises. Conscript units are regularly deployed for agricultural labor (harvesting), construction projects, and factory work — the military's economic function extends to using soldiers as free labor. Training intensity varies dramatically between elite and ordinary units; most conscripts spend more time on labor and political study than on combat training.

## The Songun Policy

Under Kim Jong-il, the principle of **Songun** (선군, military-first) was elevated to formal state doctrine, explicitly adopted in the mid-1990s during the overlapping crises of Soviet collapse, the death of Kim Il-sung (July 8, 1994), and the catastrophic **Arduous March famine** (1994–1998). Songun made military needs the organizing principle of the economy and society — resources flowed to the KPA while the civilian population starved.

Constitutionally embedded by amendment in 1998, Songun gave the military formal priority access to food, fuel, materials, and foreign currency. The **National Defense Commission**, chaired by Kim Jong-il, was elevated above the cabinet; military officers gained seats on economic and party bodies.

Kim Jong-un's consolidation of power after 2012 subtly reasserted **WPK party authority** over the KPA, demoting several military figures (including Choe Ryong-hae, who was repositioned from military to party roles) and elevating civilian party officials. This shift — from Songun back toward traditional Marxist-Leninist party supremacy over the military — reduces the military's autonomous political power while maintaining its material privileges. The practical difference for ordinary North Koreans is minimal.

## Economic Role

The KPA is not merely a fighting force; it is a **vast economic conglomerate** with tentacles extending into nearly every sector of the economy:

**Mining**: Military-controlled enterprises operate coal mines, iron ore facilities, and mineral extraction. Revenue from mineral exports — particularly to China — is channeled through military-linked trading companies.

**Construction**: The KPA's Engineering Corps builds showcase projects under Kim Jong-un's direction — the luxury Ryomyong Street apartment complex in Pyongyang (completed 2017), Masikryong ski resort (2013), and the Mirim Riding Club. Soldier-laborers provide construction manpower at no cost to the state budget.

**Agriculture**: Military farms supply food directly to KPA units, bypassing the civilian Public Distribution System. The military's better access to fuel, fertilizer, and equipment gives military-run farms higher yields than collective farms.

**Foreign currency generation**: KPA-linked trading companies engage in arms sales (North Korea is a major supplier to Middle Eastern and African states), narcotics trafficking (reduced from the 1970s–1990s peak but not eliminated), counterfeit currency production (the "supernote" US$100 bill operation), and cybercrime. These illicit revenue streams fund nuclear and missile development and elite consumption.

**Cyber operations**: The **Lazarus Group** — attributed by the US Treasury Department, FBI, and allied intelligence agencies to North Korea's **Reconnaissance General Bureau** (정찰총국, RGB), a KPA-affiliated intelligence agency — is responsible for some of the largest state-sponsored cyberattacks in history, including:
- The **2014 Sony Pictures hack** (in retaliation for the film *The Interview*)
- The **2016 Bangladesh Bank heist**: approximately US$81 million stolen via fraudulent SWIFT transactions
- The **WannaCry ransomware** campaign (May 2017): estimated 300,000 computers infected across 150 countries
- Cryptocurrency exchange thefts: the UN Panel of Experts estimated North Korea stole approximately US$630 million in cryptocurrency in 2022 alone

## Nuclear and Missile Development

The KPA's **Strategic Forces Command** (전략군), formally established April 26, 2012 — elevated by Kim Jong-un from a subordinate department to an independent service branch — directs nuclear weapons development and ballistic missile operations. It represents the apex of the KPA's technical achievement.

### Nuclear Tests

| Test | Date | Estimated Yield | Site |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | October 9, 2006 | ~1 kt (partial fizzle) | Punggye-ri |
| 2nd | May 25, 2009 | ~2–6 kt | Punggye-ri |
| 3rd | February 12, 2013 | ~6–16 kt | Punggye-ri |
| 4th | January 6, 2016 | ~6–9 kt (claimed hydrogen) | Punggye-ri |
| 5th | September 9, 2016 | ~10–30 kt | Punggye-ri |
| 6th | September 3, 2017 | ~100–250 kt (thermonuclear) | Punggye-ri |

The 6th test — conducted on September 3, 2017 — is assessed by the US, South Korean, and international intelligence community as a successful thermonuclear (hydrogen bomb) test. The seismic signature (magnitude 6.1) and the collapse of portions of Mount Mantap at the Punggye-ri test site confirm a yield in the hundreds of kilotons range.

North Korea announced a moratorium on nuclear testing in April 2018 and "destroyed" the Punggye-ri tunnels in a ceremony broadcast to foreign journalists in May 2018. However, satellite imagery and seismic monitoring suggest the test site infrastructure was preserved and may have resumed activity.

### Ballistic Missile Development

The KPA has developed an extensive ballistic missile inventory. Key systems:

| System | Range | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Hwasong-14 (ICBM) | 10,000+ km | Tested July 2017; can reach continental US |
| Hwasong-15 (ICBM) | 13,000+ km | Tested November 2017; largest pre-2022 ICBM |
| Hwasong-17 (ICBM) | 15,000+ km | Tested March 2022; largest liquid-fueled ICBM globally |
| Hwasong-18 (solid-fuel ICBM) | 15,000+ km | Tested April 2023; first solid-fuel ICBM, operationally significant |
| Pukguksong-3 (SLBM) | ~2,000 km | Tested October 2019 from submerged platform |
| KN-23/24/25 (short-range) | 400–600 km | Maneuvering warheads capable of evading THAAD |

The transition to **solid-fuel ICBMs** (Hwasong-18, tested 2023) is a major operational advance: liquid-fueled missiles require hours of fueling before launch, making them detectable by satellite; solid-fuel missiles can be launched within minutes of a command, dramatically complicating adversary preemption planning.

In November 2023, North Korea successfully launched a military reconnaissance satellite (**Malligyong-1**) into orbit, giving the KPA its first indigenous space-based intelligence capability.

## Internal Security and Repression

The KPA's internal security apparatus parallels and often rivals the civilian Ministry of State Security (보위성) in scope:

**Political commissars** (정치지도원) are embedded at every unit level from company upward, responsible for ideological surveillance and reporting. Commissar reports bypass the normal military chain of command and go directly to party political organs.

**Military Security Command** (보위사령부): counterintelligence within the KPA; monitors soldiers and officers for disloyalty, unauthorized foreign contact, or consumption of foreign media. Operates its own detention facilities.

**Political prison camp guarding**: KPA units staff and guard the **kwan-li-so** (관리소) political prison camp network — including Camp 14 (Kaechon), Camp 15 (Yodok), and Camp 16 (Hwasong) — where an estimated 80,000–120,000 political prisoners are held under conditions of systematic forced labor, torture, and starvation. The camps' existence is officially denied; UN investigations (the 2014 UN Commission of Inquiry on Human Rights in the DPRK) documented them in detail based on defector testimonies.

Defection from the KPA is treated with extreme severity: the defector's family is typically subjected to collective punishment, with three generations potentially imprisoned. This **yeon-jwa-je** (연좌제, guilt-by-association) system is one of the primary mechanisms preventing mass defection from the military.

## Modernization and Contemporary Challenges

Kim Jong-un has pursued **selective modernization** of the KPA:

**Equipment modernization**: New main battle tanks (Songun-915, displayed in 2020 parade, incorporating ERA tiles and an auto-loader suggestive of Russian design influence), improved self-propelled artillery, and modern air defense systems (KN-06, indigenous S-300 derivative). However, the bulk of the armored fleet and air force remains Soviet-era (T-54/55/62 tanks; MiG-21/29 aircraft).

**Drone development**: Multiple drone programs, including loitering munitions (kamikaze drones) and reconnaissance UAVs. In December 2022, five North Korean drones penetrated South Korean airspace, reaching as far as the Seoul Gangbuk district, in a deliberate test of South Korean air defenses.

**The Russia partnership (2023–2025)**: The war in Ukraine created a significant new military relationship. North Korea supplied Russia with artillery shells (estimated 3–5 million rounds by early 2025), ballistic missiles (KN-23 variants), and subsequently deployed an estimated 10,000–12,000 KPA soldiers to support Russian operations in Kursk Oblast in late 2024 — the first confirmed overseas combat deployment of North Korean ground forces in decades. In exchange, Russia reportedly provided advanced anti-aircraft missile systems, drone technology, and satellite intelligence. This represents a historic strategic realignment with significant implications for KPA capabilities.

**Fuel and economic constraints**: Sanctions imposed under UN Security Council Resolutions 2270 (2016), 2321 (2016), 2375 (2017), and 2397 (2017) cut North Korea's petroleum imports, constraining aircraft training hours, tank operation, and mechanized unit readiness. The Air Force is particularly degraded: pilot flight hours are estimated at 15–25 hours annually, far below the 200+ hours needed for basic combat proficiency.

## Role in Regime Maintenance

The KPA's ultimate function is **regime survival** — preserving the Kim dynasty against both internal and external threats. External defense against South Korea and its US treaty ally is the stated purpose; the internal control function is equally critical:

- The military's privileged access to food, housing, and consumer goods creates a constituency invested in regime continuation
- The threat of purge (senior officers are regularly arrested, demoted, or executed — often by anti-aircraft gun, as was reportedly used for Minister of Defence Hyon Yong-chol in 2015) enforces absolute loyalty at the elite level
- The military provides the regime's final guarantee against internal uprising — if the party's ideological control and the security services' surveillance fail, the KPA's physical coercive capacity is the last resort

The KPA's centrality — to defense, economic production, and political control — means it is not merely a military instrument but the structural skeleton of the DPRK. It will remain the institutional lynchpin of the regime as long as the totalitarian system endures.
