# State Security Department (Ministry of State Security)

The State Security Department (Bowibu in Korean; officially the Ministry of State Security, or Gukga Bowibu) is the Democratic People's Republic of Korea's primary internal-security and secret-police apparatus. It functions as the regime's pervasive intelligence and counterintelligence service, political police, and enforcement arm of the totalitarian state. The Bowibu operates with impunity, answerable only to the Supreme Leader, and is a central mechanism through which the regime maintains its monopoly on power through surveillance, terror, and the control of information.

## Origins and Evolution

Internal-security operations in the DPRK date to the state's founding in 1948, when the Soviet military occupation established security organs modeled on the Soviet NKVD/MGB. The original Political Security Bureau, operating under Soviet advisers, conducted early purges of rivals to Kim Il-sung's authority, including the "Yan'an faction" (pro-Chinese officers who had fought under Mao Zedong) and the "Soviet-Korean faction" (ethnic Koreans who had lived in the USSR).

A major reorganization in 1973 split the dedicated political-security apparatus off from ordinary criminal law enforcement, separating the secret police from the Ministry of Public (Social) Security that handled routine policing. The Bowibu as it is currently understood — a dedicated political security apparatus — took clearer shape through the late 1970s and early 1980s under Kim Jong-il's rising influence within the party apparatus.

Under Kim Jong-il, who formalized his control after Kim Il-sung's death in 1994, the Bowibu expanded and became more systematic in its penetration of society. The organization was reorganized multiple times — notably in 1996 and 2006 — partly to enhance effectiveness and partly to prevent any single security chief from accumulating independent power sufficient to become a rival. For long stretches the post of Bowibu chief was left formally vacant, with Kim Jong-il exercising direct control and first vice directors running day-to-day operations.

Under Kim Jong-un, who assumed power in December 2011, the Bowibu has been periodically purged and reorganized as part of his broader consolidation of control. A particularly dramatic illustration of the Bowibu's role was the December 2013 arrest and execution of Jang Song-thaek, Kim Jong-un's uncle and the second most powerful figure in the regime. Jang was arrested by uniformed military officers in the Politburo meeting room itself, tried, and executed within days — a purge that required the coordination of the Bowibu and the military security apparatus. The episode demonstrated both the reach of the security state and Kim Jong-un's willingness to use it against figures of any rank.

## Structure and Organization

The Bowibu operates under a hierarchical command structure, with bureaus organized by function:

- **Foreign Intelligence Bureau**: conducts espionage abroad, maintains overseas residencies under diplomatic cover, and coordinates with allied foreign intelligence services (particularly Russia's FSB and China's MSS). DPRK diplomats assigned abroad typically include Bowibu officers under cover.
- **Counterintelligence Bureau**: targets suspected foreign agents, defectors, and those who have been exposed to foreign ideas or media. This bureau maintains surveillance files on all citizens who have traveled abroad.
- **Domestic Security Bureau**: monitors the civilian population through informant networks, coordinates with party and military cadres, and enforces political loyalty. Its officers are embedded in every major workplace, school, and collective.
- **Technical Surveillance Bureau**: operates surveillance equipment, communications intercepts, and coordinates cyber operations with the Reconnaissance General Bureau (RGB).
- **Investigations Bureau**: conducts formal investigations of political crimes and manages interrogation of suspects.
- **Prison Management Bureau**: oversees the Bowibu's own interrogation centers and short-term detention facilities (distinct from the kwanliso long-term political prison system, which is managed separately but feeds from Bowibu referrals).

Field operations are conducted through regional offices in each of the DPRK's nine provinces and in Pyongyang, with subordinate units at the city, county, and neighborhood (inminban) level. Inminban (people's unit) leaders — typically a trusted resident of each apartment building or housing cluster — serve as the ground-level nodes of the Bowibu's informant network.

The organization is integrated with the [Workers' Party of Korea](workers-party-of-korea.md)'s security apparatus and the [Korean People's Army](korean-peoples-army.md)'s counterintelligence directorate (the Military Security Command), creating overlapping surveillance networks that saturate every institution.

## Surveillance and Information Control

The Bowibu's most pervasive function is the systematic surveillance of the population. Methods include:

**Informant networks**: The Bowibu maintains a vast network of civilian informants, estimated by defectors and analysts to number in the hundreds of thousands across the country. Informant networks are particularly dense in workplaces, schools, military units, and Communist Party organizations. Citizens are encouraged and sometimes coerced into participation; refusing to inform on neighbors or colleagues can itself draw suspicion. The social structure of the DPRK — with its regular mass political meetings (saenghwal chonghwa, or "life review sessions") in which individuals publicly critique themselves and others — reinforces the culture of mutual surveillance.

**Monitoring of foreign contacts**: Special attention is paid to any citizen who has contact with foreigners, defectors, or has been exposed to foreign media, Christianity, or anti-regime ideas. Those permitted to travel abroad are subjected to enhanced screening before departure and intensive ideological debriefing upon return. Family members of those who defect face collective punishment — typically reduction in songbun (social class) classification, potential relocation, and in some cases imprisonment in [kwanliso](../culture-society/kwanliso.md) facilities.

**Communications intercepts**: The Bowibu conducts systematic monitoring of telephone communications, written correspondence, and (since the 2000s) internet and mobile-phone usage. The DPRK's domestic telecommunications network (Koryolink, launched in 2008) is state-controlled and enables comprehensive surveillance. Citizens attempting to use Chinese mobile networks near the Sino-DPRK border to communicate with relatives or brokers in China are a priority target; the Bowibu deploys signal-detection equipment along the border to identify unauthorized cross-border communications.

**USB and media monitoring**: The spread of USB drives carrying South Korean dramas, films, and music constitutes a primary ideological threat in the Bowibu's assessment. Dedicated units monitor the circulation of foreign media and conduct searches of residences and devices. Possession of South Korean media can result in interrogation and prison-camp sentences; multiple defector accounts describe Bowibu officers conducting raids on homes and workplaces specifically to confiscate foreign media.

**Document and movement controls**: Movement within the DPRK requires travel permits issued by the relevant security agencies. These permits restrict population movement and allow tracking and apprehension of suspects. The permission system is particularly strict for movement to [Pyongyang](../geography/pyongyang.md), which is considered a privileged zone accessible primarily to citizens with high songbun (see also [Government Structure](government-structure.md)).

## Songbun Classification and Social Control

A distinctive feature of the DPRK security state is the songbun system — a hereditary social-classification system that stratifies the population into three broad categories (loyal, wavering, hostile) with approximately 51 sub-classifications based on family political history. The Bowibu administers and enforces songbun records, updating files in response to security incidents, political offenses, foreign contact, or acts of demonstrated loyalty.

Songbun determines access to education, employment, residence (particularly whether one may live in Pyongyang), food ration quality, and military assignment. Citizens in the "hostile" category — including families of those who fled to South Korea during the Korean War, Christians, landowners, and descendants of Japanese collaborators — face systematic discrimination and in some cases exclusion from higher education or skilled employment.

The Bowibu uses songbun files as a primary tool of investigation: when a suspect is identified, the files of their family members, workmates, and social network are reviewed. Collective punishment — the penalization of family members for an individual's political offense — is a formal and longstanding policy, designed to create social pressure against individual deviation.

## Detention and Torture

The Bowibu operates its own detention facilities (interrogation centers) separate from the kwanliso political prison-camp system. Suspects detained by the Bowibu are subjected to intensive interrogation, often involving torture, isolation, and sleep deprivation. Detention periods can last weeks or months, and conditions are severe.

Particularly notorious is the Bowibu's central interrogation facility in Pyongyang, where political prisoners and suspected foreign agents are held. Deaths during interrogation are not uncommon — defectors who have survived Bowibu detention describe beatings, stress positions, mock executions, and denial of food and sleep as standard interrogation techniques. Confessions extracted under duress are used to construct narratives of espionage, treason, or ideological contamination that are then used in political trials.

Upon completion of interrogation, detainees may be:
1. Released into the general population (often with a permanent mark on their songbun classification, restricting future opportunities);
2. Transferred to the kwanliso (political prison-camp) system for indefinite detention, where an estimated 80,000–120,000 persons were held as of the 2010s–2020s;
3. Subjected to "revolutionary" punishment short of imprisonment (forced relocation, demotion, public criticism);
4. Executed — rare for Bowibu cases but documented for high-profile political crimes.

See [Kwanliso (Political Prison Camps)](../culture-society/kwanliso.md).

## Targets and Focus Areas

The Bowibu's investigative focus has evolved over time but consistently prioritizes:

- **Suspected foreign espionage**: Targets of alleged CIA, South Korean National Intelligence Service (NIS), or Japanese intelligence operations.
- **Ideological contamination**: Individuals exposed to foreign media (South Korean television, Chinese/Russian programs), Christianity, or capitalist ideas. The DPRK estimates that tens of thousands of Korean Christians maintain their faith clandestinely; these individuals are a priority target.
- **Defection attempts and escape networks**: Tracking and neutralizing those who attempt to flee the DPRK (typically across the Tumen or Yalu rivers into China) or who assist others in escape. The Bowibu operates counterintelligence against the network of brokers, safe houses, and NGOs in China and Southeast Asia that facilitate defection.
- **Human-rights advocates and foreign contacts**: Individuals communicating with outside broadcasters (Radio Free Asia, Voice of America, BBC Korean), NGOs, or journalists.
- **Economic crimes**: The Bowibu investigates corruption, embezzlement, and illicit market activity, particularly involving high-ranking officials. This function is partly predatory — officers may extort merchants to supplement their own below-market salaries.
- **Military and party disloyalty**: Within the military and party hierarchies, the Bowibu serves as a check on the ambitions of rival power centers, monitoring potential rivals and reporting to the Supreme Leader.

## Cyber Operations and Modern Expansion

Since the 2000s, the Bowibu has expanded into digital surveillance and, in coordination with the RGB, cyber operations. The organization operates:

- Surveillance of electronic communications and monitoring of the limited internet access available to DPRK elites, diplomats, and select students at the elite Kim Il-sung University and Kim Chaek University of Technology.
- Cross-border cellular monitoring along the Sino-DPRK border using Chinese commercial equipment.
- Coordination with RGB-operated cyber-warfare units that conduct hacking and financial theft operations. The Lazarus Group, formally housed within the RGB, operates under coordination frameworks that include Bowibu counterintelligence support (see [Korean People's Army](korean-peoples-army.md)).

Hard-currency proceeds from cyber theft flow partially to security organs, providing incentives for continued expansion.

## Relationship to Other Security Agencies

The Bowibu operates alongside other security and intelligence organizations in a deliberately competitive and overlapping structure:

- **Korean People's Army's Reconnaissance General Bureau (RGB)**: the primary foreign intelligence and covert operations arm, conducting overseas assassination, abduction, cyber operations, and narcotics trafficking. The RGB operates under the KPA General Staff but reports directly to Kim Jong-un.
- **KPA Military Security Command**: monitors the military itself for political loyalty and coordinates with the Bowibu on national security matters.
- **The Workers' Party of Korea's Organization and Guidance Department (OGD)**: the party's most powerful internal-control body, overseeing cadre appointments and political loyalty throughout the party and state. The OGD, in which [Kim Yo-jong](../people/kim-yo-jong.md) (Kim Jong-un's sister) is reported to hold a senior role by the 2020s, is arguably as powerful as the Bowibu in terms of political reach.

Territorial disputes and bureaucratic competition between these organizations are common. The Supreme Leader typically mediates and ensures that no single security agency becomes independent enough to challenge the regime.

## Leadership and Political Dynamics

The Bowibu's leadership is rotated periodically to prevent the accumulation of independent power. Chiefs of the Bowibu serve at the pleasure of the Supreme Leader and are subject to purge if perceived as threats or as having failed. Under Kim Jong-un, the Bowibu leadership has changed multiple times as part of his broader consolidation of personal power (2012–2015 saw extensive reshuffling of the security services).

The organization's extensive reach and its control over information give it significant political influence, but historically the Bowibu has functioned as an instrument of the Supreme Leader's will rather than as an independent power center. Senior officers are required to demonstrate absolute, performative loyalty to the Kim family. The personality cult of [Kim Jong-un](../people/kim-jong-un.md), like those of [Kim Il-sung](../people/kim-il-sung.md) and [Kim Jong-il](../people/kim-jong-il.md) before him, permeates the Bowibu's internal culture.

## International Coordination and Partnerships

The Bowibu maintains intelligence partnerships with foreign services, particularly:

- **Russia's FSB and GRU**: collaboration on counterintelligence and foreign operations, greatly expanded since 2022. Russian intelligence has reportedly provided technical training in digital surveillance and offensive cyber operations.
- **China's MSS (Ministry of State Security)**: coordination on border security and monitoring of Korean diaspora communities in China (approximately 2 million ethnic Koreans, or Joseonjok, live in China, concentrated in the Yanbian Korean Autonomous Prefecture).
- **Smaller allies**: selective intelligence exchange with Iran, Syria, and select non-aligned countries.

## Impact on Society and the Security State

The Bowibu's pervasive surveillance and terror have profoundly shaped DPRK society. Citizens operate under constant awareness that they may be monitored, reported upon, or detained at any moment. This fear constrains speech, movement, and social interaction in ways that defectors consistently describe as pervasive and internalized from childhood.

The organization's function as both a security service and a mechanism of political control means that ordinary behaviors (listening to foreign radio, engaging in unsanctioned commerce, practicing religion) are treated as political offenses subject to Bowibu investigation and detention. The regime's stability, despite the acute economic hardship of successive [economic crises](../economy/economic-system-and-famine.md), is sustained in significant part by the Bowibu's pervasive enforcement network.
