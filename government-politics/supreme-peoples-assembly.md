# Supreme People's Assembly

**Category**: Government & Politics  
**Key figures**: Choe Ryong-hae (current Standing Committee president), Kim Il-sung (founding Premier 1948–1972, then President 1972–1994), Kim Tu-bong (first Standing Committee/Presidium chairman, 1948–1957), Kim Yong-nam (Standing Committee president 1998–2019)

## Summary

The Supreme People's Assembly (SPA; Korean: 최고인민회의, *Chego Inmin Hoeui*) is the nominal legislature of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea. It comprises approximately 687 deputies elected to five-year terms and is constitutionally defined as "the highest organ of state power." Established in 1948 as the governing body of the provisional DPRK government, it remains the apex of the regime's official constitutional structure while exercising no real independent authority. In theory the SPA holds supreme state power—passing laws, electing the Supreme Leader, amending the constitution, approving budgets, and ratifying international treaties. In practice it is a rubber-stamp body that unanimously approves decisions already made by the Supreme Leader and the Workers' Party of Korea leadership.

## Historical Development

The SPA was formally constituted on September 9, 1948, the same day the DPRK was proclaimed. Its first session confirmed Kim Il-sung as Premier and adopted the DPRK's founding constitution, which was modeled closely on the 1936 Soviet "Stalin" Constitution. Deputies were "elected" in the Soviet fashion with pre-selected slates, unanimous results, and turnout figures exceeding 99 percent. The assembly met more frequently in the early years of the state (1948–1950), before the Korean War disrupted governance.

During the Korean War (1950–1953), the SPA continued to hold sessions, sometimes convening in Pyongyang under bombardment, sometimes relocated to the north. The assembly formally ratified war measures, mobilization decrees, and armistice provisions, though all substantive decisions were made by Kim Il-sung and his military command. After the armistice, the SPA ratified reconstruction plans and economic goals, again confirming decisions already made through the party apparatus.

A significant structural change occurred with the 1972 constitution, which replaced the position of Premier (head of government) with President (head of state), consolidated power more formally in Kim Il-sung, and created the Central People's Committee—a small group that exercised executive authority between SPA sessions. The SPA under the 1972 constitution became even more nominal; real power was explicitly located in the party, the military, and the personal authority of the Supreme Leader.

After Kim Il-sung's death in July 1994, Kim Jong-il refused to take the title of President, leaving that position formally vacant as a mark of deference to his father, who was posthumously declared "Eternal President." Kim Jong-il instead held power through the National Defence Commission (NDC), which was elevated to the supreme state organ in constitutional amendments in 1998. The SPA ratified these amendments unanimously and with applause.

Under Kim Jong-un, further constitutional revision in 2016 abolished the National Defence Commission and replaced it with the State Affairs Commission (SAC), headed by the Supreme Leader. The 2016 amendments formally designated the SAC as the "highest general policy guidance organ of state power," effectively subordinating the SPA—still nominally the highest state organ—to the new commission structure. The SPA ratified these changes to its own diminution in a session lasting hours.

## Composition and "Elections"

The SPA is divided into electoral constituencies across all provinces and cities of the DPRK. Each constituency nominates a single candidate, approved by the party, who runs unopposed. Voter turnout is reported at 99.97–100 percent in every election. Deputies include senior party officials, military commanders, factory managers, farmers selected as model workers, scientists, and cultural figures—a mix that provides the appearance of broad social representation while ensuring all members are loyal to the regime.

The 14th Supreme People's Assembly, elected on March 10, 2019, remained the most recent as of 2025, with the regime reporting 99.99 percent turnout and unanimous support for all candidates. In the previous (13th) election of March 2014, Kim Jong-un himself stood as a deputy for the Mount Paektu constituency, a symbolically charged district tied to the founding mythology of the Kim family; in the 2019 election, notably, his name was absent from the published lists of candidates and elected deputies for the first time—a break with precedent that state media did not explain.

Deputies are drawn from several overlapping categories:
- **Senior Workers' Party of Korea officials**: Politburo members and Central Committee members who hold SPA seats alongside their party positions
- **Military commanders**: Senior Korean People's Army officers, including members of the Central Military Commission
- **Technocrats and administrators**: Ministers, provincial party secretaries, managers of major state enterprises
- **Symbolic representatives**: Model workers, farmers, and cultural figures selected to present an image of popular participation

## Sessions and Procedures

The SPA convenes in full session (plenary) typically two to three times per year. Sessions last one to three days. The agenda is determined by the Standing Committee and ratified by the Workers' Party secretariat before sessions open. Votes are taken by show of hands and are invariably unanimous. Speeches by deputies follow prepared texts approved in advance. No dissent, debate in the Western parliamentary sense, or contested voting is recorded.

A typical SPA session includes:
1. A report on economic targets and their fulfilment by the Cabinet Premier
2. A report on the state budget (revenues and expenditures) by the Finance Minister
3. Ratification of laws or constitutional amendments already drafted by party organs
4. Appointment confirmation of senior officials whose positions have already been decided
5. Pledges of loyalty and organizational resolutions

The SPA does not publish verbatim transcripts; official North Korean media (KCNA, Rodong Sinmun) report sessions in summary form, emphasizing "unanimous approval" and expressions of devotion to the Supreme Leader.

## The Standing Committee

The Standing Committee of the SPA (상임위원회) serves as the assembly's executive body between sessions. It comprises a chairman, several vice-chairmen, a secretary, and additional members. The Standing Committee meets more frequently than the full assembly and issues regulations, decrees, and interpretations of laws on the SPA's behalf. In practice, the Standing Committee's decisions reflect instructions from the party apparatus rather than independent deliberation.

The chairmanship of the SPA Standing Committee (styled president of the Presidium after 1998) has been held by senior regime figures who occupy the position as an honorary reward for political loyalty rather than as a seat of actual power. Notable holders include:
- **Kim Tu-bong** (1948–1957), a Yan'an-faction communist and the first chairman of the Presidium, later purged
- **Choe Yong-gon** (1957–1972), a veteran of the anti-Japanese guerrilla struggle and close ally of Kim Il-sung
- **Kim Yong-nam** (1998–2019), the durable technocrat who served as the DPRK's nominal head of state and chief ceremonial representative abroad for two decades
- **Choe Ryong-hae** (2019–present), a senior Politburo member and former military official who has cycled through multiple senior positions

## Constitutional Role and Key Legislative Acts

Despite its ceremonial nature, the SPA has formally enacted several constitutionally significant acts:

- **1972 Constitutional Revision**: Replaced the 1948 constitution, creating the presidency and the Central People's Committee, concentrating power in Kim Il-sung's person
- **1998 Constitutional Revision**: Elevated the National Defence Commission as the supreme organ of state power under Kim Jong-il; declared Kim Il-sung "Eternal President"
- **2009 Constitutional Revision**: Further consolidated military-first (Songun) policy in constitutional language
- **2012 Constitutional Revision**: Added explicit reference to North Korea's status as a nuclear weapons state following the 2009 nuclear test
- **2016 Constitutional Revision**: Created the State Affairs Commission as the supreme policy organ, replacing the National Defence Commission and subordinating the SPA
- **2024 Constitutional Revision**: Acting on the doctrine Kim Jong-un announced at the December 2023 party plenum and elaborated at the January 2024 SPA session, moved to remove language about eventual Korean reunification and to define North Korea and South Korea as "two separate hostile states," a fundamental revision to the DPRK's founding ideological mission

## Significance

The Supreme People's Assembly embodies one of the fundamental contradictions of the DPRK: the regime maintains a constitutional façade of parliamentary representation and collective decision-making while wielding absolute power through the Supreme Leader and the party. The existence of the SPA serves multiple regime purposes: it provides international legitimacy by appearing to operate within a legal and constitutional framework; it offers a venue for the ritualized mobilization and display of loyalty from the broader elite; and it allows the state to project an image of collective governance while maintaining complete subordination to the Kim family.

The assembly's irrelevance to actual governance has only grown over three generations of Kim rule, as power has become ever more concentrated in the Supreme Leader's person. The post-2016 constitutional amendments—which formally subordinated the SPA to the State Affairs Commission—represent the regime's move to dispense with even the pretense of the assembly's autonomy. The 2023–2024 repudiation of reunification was perhaps the most substantive matter the SPA has handled in decades, signaling a genuine strategic shift rather than mere procedural ratification; yet even this decision was made by Kim Jong-un and merely formalized by the assembly.

The SPA's persistence, despite its complete lack of actual authority, illustrates the regime's need to maintain revolutionary legitimacy derived from Soviet and early socialist models even as the substance of governance has been replaced by hereditary totalitarian autocracy.
